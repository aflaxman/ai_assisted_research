Replication · compartmental modelling

Replicating Brazil's Yellow Fever Model

The paper's climate conclusion holds. Its published parameter table does not produce it.

Sadeghieh, Sargeant, Greer, Berke, Dueymes, Gachon, Ogden & Ng (2021). “Yellow fever virus outbreak in Brazil under current and future climate.” Infectious Disease Modelling 6:664–677. doi:10.1016/j.idm.2021.04.002

  • Replicates Fewer cases as Brazil warms Cumulative incidence falls 33% by 2071–2100 under the high-emissions pathway. The paper reports 31%.
  • Replicates Shorter outbreaks Outbreak duration contracts with temperature, driven by mosquito mortality, as the paper argues.
  • Does not replicate A falling epidemic peak Under one consistent parameterisation the peak is flat to slightly higher, not 9–20% lower.
  • Does not replicate The published parameter table Run as printed, Table 1 yields 30,175 cases against the paper's own 1,254.

01 · The data

Starting from the figure, because the data were never published

The paper fits weekly confirmed yellow fever cases from Brazil's 2017/18 season, but publishes no data file. I digitised the eighteen red markers in its Figure 3. Three independent checks say the digitisation is exact: the values sum to 1,259, the peak is 214, and the series runs 15 weeks before hitting zero. Those are precisely the observed figures the paper's own Table 4 reports.

Everything below runs through camdl, a compiled domain-specific language for compartmental models. The paper's nine-compartment vector–host system is transcribed from its Appendix A.1 and type-checked before any simulation runs.

02 · The first problem

Table 1 overshoots the paper's own result 24-fold

Before refitting anything, I ran the model with every parameter exactly as Table 1 and Table 2 print them. It produces 30,175 cases peaking in week 2. The paper's own fitted output, on the facing page, is 1,254 cases peaking in week 4.

Two independent integrators agree on this, so it is not a numerical artefact: camdl's ODE backend and a hand-written Runge–Kutta integration return the same curve.

03 · Why

Three published values cannot all be right

The overshoot is not one typo. Tracing it back finds three separate inconsistencies inside the paper's own tables.

The fatality rate contradicts Table 4

Table 1 gives a death rate of 2.38 per week against a recovery rate of 0.82, which implies that 74% of symptomatic cases die. The paper's Table 4 reports 426 deaths from 1,254 cases, which is 34%. A death rate near 0.42 per week is what reproduces the paper's own figure, and that is the value used throughout here.

The initial conditions cannot produce a week-4 peak

Table 2 seeds the outbreak with 995 infectious mosquitoes and 150 exposed ones. An infectious seed is already at its maximum at time zero, so incidence can only fall from week 1. Rescaling the transmission rate until the case total matches still peaks in week 2. Reproducing a rising limb requires the seed to sit in the exposed class instead, with the infectious count near zero.

The mosquito-to-human transmission rate cannot be estimated at all

This one is structural rather than arithmetic, and it is the most consequential.

The likelihood has a flat ridge. Over the course of this outbreak the susceptible human and mosquito pools barely move, so the system is effectively linear in the infected compartments. Multiply β1 by any constant c while dividing β2, Y0 and Z0 by the same c, and every human compartment is unchanged. Only the three products β1β2, β1Y0 and β1Z0 are identified. The paper reports β1 and β2 as separately estimated point values.

04 · Refitting

Pinning the ridge makes the problem well posed

With β1 fixed at the paper's published value, the remaining quantities are identified and the model fits the season closely. camdl's deterministic optimiser over 64 Latin-hypercube starts is the direct analogue of the paper's own least-squares procedure, differing in using a Poisson likelihood for counts rather than a sum of squares.

I ran two baselines, because the paper's own choice of mosquito parameters turns out to matter a great deal. Baseline A takes the three climate-sensitive mosquito parameters from Table 1, which is what the paper's baseline run uses. Baseline B takes them from the Table 3 temperature polynomials evaluated at the baseline 23 °C.

05 · The confound

The baseline and the scenarios do not share a parameterisation

The paper's climate comparison sets its baseline from Table 1 and its scenarios from the Table 3 polynomials. Those two sources disagree sharply at the same 23 °C baseline temperature: the polynomials give a mosquito death rate of 0.305 against Table 1's 0.36, a development rate of 0.37 against 0.58, and an extrinsic incubation rate of 0.217 against 0.54.

So the paper's headline step from baseline to the 2011–2040 period mixes a temperature change with a change of parameter source. The toggle below separates them. Variant A follows the paper's procedure; variant B holds one parameterisation throughout, so a difference is a temperature effect.

06 · The mechanism

The decline has a closed form

Because the outbreak is driven by a decaying cohort of infected mosquitoes rather than by sustained transmission, the total number of human cases is proportional to the infectious-mosquito-weeks that cohort delivers. For an exposed mosquito facing death rate μ and extrinsic incubation rate λ2, that integral is:

λ2 ÷ [ μ (λ2 + μ) ]

Warming raises both rates. Faster incubation helps the virus; higher mortality hurts it; mortality wins because it enters twice. This expression predicts the simulated change in cumulative incidence to the second decimal in all six scenarios, which is why the paper's central result survives every problem above. Drag the temperature to see it.

07 · What the model is

This is a spillover model, not a transmission model

In every parameterisation tried here, the basic reproduction number sits far below one. No human-amplified transmission is sustained; the epidemic is the decay of its initial mosquito cohort. When the three mosquito parameters are made temperature-consistent, the optimiser drives human-to-mosquito transmission to its lower bound, which is to say it finds no evidence of feedback at all.

The paper's own Table 6 says the same thing, and it is worth being precise about how. Its vaccination sweep is almost perfectly linear in the susceptible fraction: successive 10-point steps change peak incidence by 96, 97, 96, 96, 96, 96 cases. A model with real transmission feedback would curve. A model where cases are proportional to the number of people available to be bitten is a straight line.

Re-running that sweep with the paper's published β2 reproduces the linearity and lands within 8% of its cumulative incidence at every level.

This is not a modelling error so much as an unflagged consequence of the paper's third simplifying assumption. Brazil's 2017/18 epidemic was sylvatic: Haemagogus mosquitoes cycling the virus through non-human primates, spilling into people near forest edges. The paper states that it excludes non-human primates as intermediate hosts, then fits a human–mosquito transmission model to a human–mosquito epidemic that did not occur. The fit resolves the tension by setting the feedback to nothing and letting an exogenous seed carry the whole outbreak.

The climate conclusion survives this, because it depends only on mosquito survival and incubation. Any claim about transmission intensity, vaccination thresholds or herd immunity does not.

08 · One more thing

A slip in Table 4

Recomputing the percentage changes in the paper's Table 4 from the paper's own absolute values reproduces five of the six death figures exactly. The sixth does not.

294 deaths against a baseline of 426 is a 31.0% decrease. The table prints 40.0%. The cumulative-incidence percentage on the same row is correct, so this is a transcription slip in one cell rather than a different baseline.

And the peak baseline is 184, not 185

A second, smaller inconsistency sits in the same table. All six of its peak percentages are computed against a baseline peak of 184, which reproduces every printed value to the decimal. Table 4 prints that baseline peak as 185 in the row above it. Table 6 gives the same quantity, the fitted model at 80% immunity, as 184. The comparisons on this page use 184.

Neither slip changes a conclusion. Both are the kind of thing a reader cannot catch without re-running the model, which is the argument for publishing one.

09 · Reproducing this

What to run

  1. The model is one file, yf_brazil.camdl, transcribed from Appendix A.1. camdl check type-checks it, including the dimensions of every rate.
  2. Three fit configurations cover the unpinned fit, baseline A and baseline B. camdl fit run stores each result content-addressed by its inputs.
  3. run_replication.py drives every scenario, the sensitivity analysis and the vaccination sweep through camdl simulate, and writes the tables this page reads.

One reparameterisation. The paper writes transmission as β1SZ, which gives β1 units of inverse count-weeks. camdl's dimension lattice has no inverse-count literal, so the same flow is written per-capita against a fixed reference population, β1S(Z/M). The dynamics are identical; only the units of the two fitted constants change. Published values map to β1 = 218.69 and β2 = 2.09×10−4 per week.

Replication run on camdl 0.1.0+e8219d1, ODE backend, fixed-step RK4 at dt = 1/16 week. Case data digitised from the paper's Figure 3 and validated against its Table 4. Deterministic fits over 64 Latin-hypercube starts; the reported optimum was reached independently by many starts and agrees to six decimal places.
