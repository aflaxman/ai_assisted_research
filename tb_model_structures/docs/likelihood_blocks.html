Fitting two model families to overlapping blocks of data

Why the classic latent models and the spectrum models in this project are not fit to a single shared likelihood, and what that means for the comparison.

The short version. A classic latent-TB model has one disease state; a spectrum model has three (minimal, subclinical, clinical). Much of the best natural-history data describes transitions among those three states, which a one-disease-state model literally cannot speak to. So the likelihood is split into blocks, and each family is scored on the blocks it can address. The families are compared head-to-head only on the blocks they share.

The problem: the two families do not describe the same quantities

The twelve classic structures catalogued by Menzies and colleagues all end at a single compartment I — active TB disease. They differ in how a newly infected person reaches I (one latent stage or two, reinfection, time-varying reactivation), but once there, disease is a single undifferentiated state.

The spectrum models resolve that one state into three along a bidirectional axis: minimal (pathology, not yet bacteriologically positive, non-infectious), subclinical (bacteriologically positive, infectious, no symptoms) and clinical (positive, infectious, symptomatic). People move forward and backward along it.

The richest modern natural-history dataset — 22 untreated cohorts assembled by Richards and colleagues — measures exactly those within-spectrum moves: the fraction who go minimal → subclinical over two years, who regress clinical → minimal, and so on. A classic model has no subclinical and no minimal state, so it has nothing to predict for those rows. Forcing it to (by declaring, say, I = subclinical + clinical) would be an extra modelling assumption layered on top of the structure, not a property of the structure itself. That would make the comparison measure the assumption, not the model.

How the comparison is read

Each structure is calibrated to the blocks it can address, and the fit is scored with pointwise log-likelihoods so any subset can be compared after the fact (LOO / WAIC in ArviZ). The three research questions then read off different slices:

  • (a) Fit to natural-history data. The head-to-head is on the shared block: the infectious-band converter cohorts, which both families map onto cleanly. Blocks (i-minimal) and (iii) show what the spectrum family buys with its extra states, but they are not a classic-vs-spectrum contest because the classic family cannot enter them.
  • (b) Subclinical fraction. A quantity only the spectrum family produces directly; the classic family's implied value requires the same I = Sub+Clin assumption and is reported as such.
  • (c) Screening impact. Here the families diverge most: active screening detects bacteriologically positive disease regardless of symptoms, so a spectrum model can yield asymptomatic (subclinical) cases while a classic model's single symptomatic I cannot. That is a structural prediction, read from the transmission embedding, not the cohort fit.

Honest asymmetries

These are deliberate and stated wherever a result depends on them.

  • The classic family is pinned on duration and case fatality; the spectrum family is pinned on the transition data. Both are the natural choice for each family, but they are not the same constraint, so a parameter like “duration of infectious disease” is a target for one family and a prediction for the other. The spectrum prediction is compared to the same target descriptively.
  • For the minimal-band converter cohorts, the classic family is excluded rather than forced, because radiographic minimal disease precedes the active disease a classic I represents.
  • The classic structures are compared to each other across all twelve; the spectrum family contributes three variants (with and without the minimal state, with and without self-clearance of infection) so the family boundary itself is visible.
