## 1. Cochrane evidence and its population: effect sizes, who was eligible, when they were enrolled, subgroups, trial countries, and the rehabilitation-service reviews

### Takeaway
The stroke-unit effect (odds ratio about 0.75 for death, for death or dependency, and for death or institutional care at a median of one year) was measured only in people already admitted to hospital, most of them randomised within 7 days of onset but in a sizeable minority at 2 weeks or later, so the effect size attaches to "admitted and alive at enrolment", not to all incident strokes. Benefits did not differ by age, sex or stroke type, appeared larger in severe stroke, and rest on 29 trials in 12 countries, of which only the Chinese and Brazilian trials were outside high-income settings.

### Cited Findings

#### Organised inpatient (stroke unit) care: Langhorne and Ramachandra 2020, CD000197.pub4 (PMID 32324916, PMC7197653)
- Size and search date: "We included 29 trials (5902 participants) that compared organised inpatient (stroke unit) care with an alternative service: 20 trials (4127 participants) compared organised (stroke unit) care with a general ward, six trials (982 participants) compared different forms of organised (stroke unit) care, and three trials (793 participants) incorporated more than one comparison." Searches ran to 2 April 2019. — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Headline odds ratios, all "at the end of scheduled follow-up (median one year)": "poor outcome (odds ratio (OR) 0.77, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.69 to 0.87; moderate-quality evidence), death (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.88; moderate-quality evidence), death or institutional care (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.85; moderate-quality evidence), and death or dependency (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.85; moderate-quality evidence)." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Trials and participants behind each outcome (full text): poor outcome "26 trials (5336 participants)"; death "all 29 trials (5902 participants)", with "median follow-up 12 months; range 6 weeks to 12 months"; death or institutional care "24 trials (4887 participants)" (the summary-of-findings table lists 25 studies for the same 4887 participants); death or dependency "24 trials (4854 participants)". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4, full text in PMC](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Absolute risks in the summary-of-findings table (assumed control risk, then risk with stroke-unit care, per 1000): poor outcome 577 and 517; death 219 and 199; death or institutional care 405 and 345; death or dependency 609 and 549. The abstract states this as "for every 100 participants receiving stroke unit care, this equates to two extra survivors, six more living at home, and six more living independently." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Longer follow-up: at five years (three trials, 1139 participants) "death 0.74 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.94)"; at ten years "death OR 0.66 (95% CI 0.43 to 1.03)". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- By service model, direct comparison with a general ward: stroke ward, poor outcome OR 0.78 (0.68 to 0.91), death "OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.63 to 0.90" (15 trials, 3523 participants), "with no subgroup interaction between the different types of stroke ward" (comprehensive versus rehabilitation); mobile stroke team, poor outcome OR 0.80 (0.52 to 1.22) and death OR 1.08 (0.71 to 1.63), two trials, 438 participants; mixed rehabilitation ward, poor outcome OR 0.65 (0.47 to 0.90) and death OR 0.91 (0.58 to 1.42), six trials, 630 participants. Network meta-analysis against a general ward: "stroke ward - OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.89 ...; mobile stroke team - OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.34 ...; mixed rehabilitation ward - OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.95". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)

#### Who was eligible, and when after onset they were enrolled
- Eligibility: "Any person admitted to hospital who had suffered a stroke was eligible. We recorded the delay between stroke onset and hospital admission but did not use this as an exclusion criterion. We used a clinical definition of stroke: focal neurological deficit due to cerebrovascular disease, excluding subarachnoid haemorrhage and subdural haematoma." The summary-of-findings table gives "Patient or population: adults with acute stroke" and "Settings: hospital". The plain-language summary: "Participants who were recruited had had a recent stroke and required admission to hospital." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Acute versus delayed admission to the unit: "Of the 29 included trials, 23 incorporated rehabilitation lasting several weeks if required: 17 of these units admitted participants acutely, and eight after a delay of one or two weeks." (The source's own counts, 17 plus 8, do not sum to 23.) The review's taxonomy includes "Rehabilitation stroke units that accept patients after a delay, usually of seven days or longer, and focus on rehabilitation." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Enrolment window per trial, from the "Characteristics of included studies" table (wording abridged from the Participants rows): Athens 1995, "admitted to emergency department within 24 hours of symptoms", excluding TIA or recurrent stroke; Beijing 2004, "admitted to hospital with first or recurrent stroke"; Birmingham 1972, "within 2 weeks of stroke onset"; Dover 1984, "up to 9 weeks after stroke onset (majority within 3 weeks)"; Edinburgh 1980, Goteborg-Ostra 1988, Goteborg-Sahlgren 1994, Helsinki 1995, Joinville 2003, Kuopio 1985, Montreal 1985, Perth 1997, Tampere 1993, all "within 7 days"; Groningen 2003, "admitted within 24 hours"; Guangdong 2009 and Huaihua 2004, "randomised on admission"; Guangdong 2008 and Hunan 2007, "timing of randomisation unclear"; Illinois 1966, "up to 1 year after stroke onset"; Manchester 2003, "within 5 days of symptoms"; New South Wales 2014, "within previous 24 to 48 hours"; New York 1962, "up to 2 months after stroke"; Newcastle 1993, "within 3 days"; Nottingham 1996, "at 2 weeks after stroke onset"; Orpington 1993 and 1995, patients who had "survived for 2 weeks"; Orpington 2000, "from a community stroke register", intermediate severity; Svendborg 1995, "within 8 days of symptoms"; Trondheim 1991, "within 7 days (usually within 24 hours)". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4, full text in PMC](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Several trials selected on fitness for rehabilitation or severity: for example Birmingham 1972 "Able to tolerate active rehabilitation", Edinburgh 1980 "Stroke of moderate severity", Trondheim 1991 "Exclusion of deeply unconscious patients and those previously residing in a nursing home", Orpington 1995 "People who had a poor prognosis 2 weeks after stroke". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- The authors' own statement on reach of the intervention among the admitted: "Although essentially all stroke patients would be eligible for admission to a stroke unit, only a small proportion would be eligible for treatment with re-perfusion therapies even in the most established acute centres". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)

#### Subgroups (age, sex, severity, subtype)
- Abstract: "Outcomes appeared to be independent of patient age, sex, initial stroke severity, stroke type, and duration of follow-up." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Full text is more qualified: "the magnitude of benefit seemed greater for participants with more severe stroke (the only significant subgroup interactions were for stroke severity: P = 0.004). However, stroke unit benefits are apparent across a range of participant subgroups (i.e. age, sex, initial stroke severity, and stroke type)." And: "Apparent benefits were seen in people of both sexes, younger and older than 75 years, with ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, and across a range of stroke severities." Each subgroup analysis "included data from at least nine trials (at least 1111 participants)". Severity was "dependency at the time of randomisation (usually within one week of the index stroke)". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- For mild stroke: "People with a mild stroke appeared to benefit from stroke unit care when death or dependency was the chosen outcome (Figure 4), but this effect was less certain for the outcomes of death and death or institutional care (data not shown)." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Practice conclusion: "There are no firm grounds for restricting access according to a person's age, sex, stroke severity, or pathological stroke type (i.e. ischaemic or haemorrhagic)." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)

#### Countries and applicability to low- and middle-income settings
- "The review now summarises data from a total of 29 trials (5902 participants) from 12 countries in Asia, Australia, Europe, North America, and South America. A majority of trials have been performed in high-income countries. Observational studies indicate that stroke units are likely to be effective in low- or middle-income countries (Langhorne 2018; Seenan 2007; Urimubenshi 2017), but there is a shortage of information from randomised clinical trials (Langhorne 2012)." — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Chinese evidence is incomplete: "we did not search Chinese databases, and we were unable to classify or obtain useable outcome data for 7 of the 11 Chinese studies that we did identify". The included Chinese trials are Beijing 2004, Guangdong 2008, Guangdong 2009, Huaihua 2004 and Hunan 2007; the Brazilian trial is Joinville 2003 (35 patients in the stroke-unit arm). — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Era: "most would still have been undertaken in an era without routine access to re-perfusion therapies". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Observational estimate across income levels (INTERSTROKE, 12,342 patients, 108 hospitals, 28 countries, enrolled 2007 to 2015): "across all countries, irrespective of economic level, access to a stroke unit was associated with improved use of investigations and treatments, access to other rehabilitation services, and improved survival without severe dependency (odds ratio [OR] 1·29; 95% CI 1·14-1·44; all p<0·0001), which was independent of patient casemix characteristics and other measures of care." — [Langhorne et al., Lancet 2018;391:2019, PMID 29864018](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30802-X)
- Observational estimate in China (Bigdata Observatory Platform for Stroke of China; 20 hospitals in 16 provinces; 24,090 admissions treated in conventional wards in 2013 to 2015 against 21,332 treated in stroke units in 2017 to 2019): in-hospital death "2.93 vs 4.58% ... odd ratio {OR}, 0.72{95% CI, 0.61 to 0.82}". This is a before-and-after comparison. — [Tu et al., J Neurol 2021;268:4213, PMID 33885974](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-021-10532-7)

#### Rehabilitation-service reviews likely meant by "stroke rehabilitation centres"
- Early supported discharge (Langhorne and Baylan 2017, CD000443.pub4, PMID 28703869). Eligible trials: "Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) recruiting stroke patients in hospital". "We included 17 trials, recruiting 2422 participants ... Participants tended to be a selected elderly group of stroke survivors with moderate disability." Length of stay: "mean difference (MD) -5.5; 95% confidence interval (CI) -3 to -8 days". Death or dependency "at the end of scheduled follow-up (median 6 months; range 3 to 12) was OR 0.80 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.95 ...) which equates to five fewer adverse outcomes per 100 patients receiving ESD". Death "OR 1.04 (95% CI 0.77 to 1.40 ...)"; death or institutional care "OR 0.75 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.96 ...)". "Stroke patients with mild to moderate disability at baseline showed greater reductions in death or dependency than those with more severe stroke (subgroup interaction at P = 0.04)." — [Cochrane CD000443.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000443.pub4)
- Physical rehabilitation approaches (Todhunter-Brown et al. 2025, CD001920.pub4, PMID 39932103; the previous version is CD001920.pub3, PMID 24756870). "we included 267 studies (21,838 participants). Studies were conducted in 36 countries, with half (133/267) in China." Against no rehabilitation: "physical rehabilitation may improve IADL (standardised mean difference (SMD) 1.32, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.08 to 1.56; 52 studies, 5403 participants; low-certainty evidence) and motor function (SMD 1.01, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.22; 50 studies, 5669 participants; low-certainty evidence)". Population: "adult participants with a clinical diagnosis of stroke". Outcomes are function scales, not death or dependency. Searches to November 2022. — [Cochrane CD001920.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001920.pub4)
- Therapy-based rehabilitation services at home (Outpatient Service Trialists 2003, CD002925, PMID 12535444). Population: "stroke patient resident in the community within one year of stroke onset/ discharge from hospital". "We identified a heterogeneous group of 14 trials including 1617 patients. Therapy-based rehabilitation services reduced the odds of a poor outcome (Peto odds ratio 0.72 (95% CI 0.57 to 0.92; P = 0.009) ... For every 100 stroke patients resident in the community receiving therapy-based rehabilitation services, 7 (95% CI 2 to 11) patients would be spared a poor outcome, assuming 37.5% would have had a poor outcome with no treatment." Searches to November 2001. — [Cochrane CD002925](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002925)
- Inpatient rehabilitation units: within CD000197.pub4 the rehabilitation stroke ward is a sub-type of "stroke ward", and the review found "benefits were apparent for both comprehensive and rehabilitation stroke wards, indicating that organised stroke unit care is of benefit in both acute and rehabilitation phases of care. Mixed rehabilitation wards also showed some benefit in reducing dependency." The mixed rehabilitation ward results are given above. — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)

### Inferences
- Country assignment of the 29 trials is mine, from place names: United Kingdom 9 (Birmingham, Dover, Edinburgh, Manchester, Newcastle, Nottingham, Orpington 1993, 1995, 2000), China 5, Finland 3 (Helsinki, Kuopio, Tampere), Sweden 2, United States 2 (Illinois, New York), Australia 2 (New South Wales, Perth), and one each in Greece, the Netherlands, Brazil, Canada, Denmark and Norway. That is 12 countries, which matches the review's count. On this reading 6 of 29 trials come from middle-income countries (China and Brazil, both upper-middle-income), and none from a low-income or lower-middle-income country.
- Counting the Participants rows, seven of the 29 trials enrolled at or beyond one to two weeks after onset (Birmingham within 2 weeks, Dover up to 9 weeks, Illinois up to 1 year, New York up to 2 months, Nottingham at 2 weeks, Orpington 1993 and Orpington 1995 at 2 weeks), which is close to the review's "eight after a delay of one or two weeks"; Orpington 2000 drew acute cases from a community register. A pooled effect that mixes acute and post-acute enrolment is conditional on surviving to enrolment, so applying OR 0.76 for death to everyone who reaches a hospital door slightly overstates the eligible base where early in-hospital deaths are common.
- The matching denominator for the second factor is therefore "incident strokes admitted to a hospital bed within roughly the first week, excluding subarachnoid haemorrhage". Three boundary cases need a decision: strokes seen in an emergency department and sent home, strokes occurring in patients already in hospital (eligible in the trials' sense, but not "reaching" hospital), and strokes admitted only late for rehabilitation (eligible in a third of the trials).
- The control-arm risks (death 21.9%, death or dependency 60.9% at about one year) describe admitted trial patients in mostly high-income hospitals, in trials labelled 1962 to 2014. Absolute gains per 100 admitted (two deaths, six dependencies) will differ where case fatality among the admitted is higher, as INTERSTROKE reports for LMIC hospitals.
- Early supported discharge and home-based therapy act on survivors after the acute phase and show no mortality effect (ESD death OR 1.04), so a "rehabilitation centre" arm cannot borrow the stroke-unit mortality odds ratio; its eligible population is hospital survivors with mild to moderate disability.

### Gaps
- The subgroup odds ratios (Figure 4 of CD000197.pub4) are an image in PMC and were not extracted; only the interaction P value for severity (0.004) is in the text.
- I did not find a Cochrane review devoted to inpatient stroke rehabilitation units as distinct from CD000197; my search was limited to the titles named in the brief plus PubMed keyword searches, so absence is not established.
- Could not verify: full texts of CD000443.pub4 and CD002925 (PMC returned abstracts only; the proportion of hospitalised patients eligible for ESD was therefore not retrieved); CD001920.pub3 beyond its bibliographic record.

## 2. Modelling precedents: how earlier analyses defined and sourced the share of strokes that could receive hospital-based care, and baseline coverage

### Takeaway
None of the global scale-up analyses I retrieved contains an explicit, empirically sourced "fraction of incident strokes admitted to hospital". The WHO-CHOICE family applies a fixed coverage (80% or 95%) to "hospitalized patients with stroke" against a null scenario, NCD Countdown 2030 and DCP3 leave acute stroke care out, the WSO-Lancet Neurology Commission holds care constant, and the one analysis with an explicit utilisation term (Kruk et al.) assumed 88% to 92% from a 2002-03 survey question about heart disease, asthma or minor surgery.

### Inferences
- The identical treated-case counts for stroke-unit care (all stroke) and aspirin (ischaemic stroke only) in Ortegon's appendix 7 suggest that the 2012 WHO-CHOICE model applied 80% coverage to one regional count of acute strokes without separate terms for hospital admission or subtype. Dividing by 0.8 gives about 209,900 strokes a year in AfrE and 955,600 in SearD as the implied population in need. This is my reading of the table, not a statement in the paper.
- In all WHO-CHOICE variants the "reaches hospital" step is folded into coverage: the label says "hospitalized patients", the model scales coverage against a null. A team that multiplies incident strokes by an admitted fraction and then by a coverage gain is therefore more explicit than the WHO precedent, and the product (admitted fraction times stroke-unit share among the admitted) is the quantity comparable to WHO-CHOICE "coverage".
- Two generations of WHO-CHOICE disagree by more than an order of magnitude on health impact: 43 to 54 DALYs averted per million per year at 80% coverage (2012) against 1.4 to 1.8 healthy life years per million per year at 95% coverage (2022). The metrics differ (discounted, age-weighted DALYs over a 10-year programme against undiscounted healthy life years averaged over 100 years), but not obviously by a factor of 30. The 2022 annex ranks stroke units below thrombolysis in health gain (1.6 against 18.3 in low-income countries), which is the reverse of Gilligan 2005 (46 against 11 per 1000 cases) and of the Cochrane authors' remark that nearly all admitted patients are eligible for a stroke unit. I could not find the technical brief that would explain the eligible population or effect size used for CV7; any global estimate built on the Cochrane odds ratio should expect to be compared with this figure.
- Kruk's 88% to 92% is the only explicit utilisation number for stroke in the global literature I retrieved, and it is a weak analogue of acute admission: the survey item asks about any facility contact, in- or outpatient, among people who perceived a need for care for heart disease, asthma or minor surgery, was fielded in 2002-03 in 40 LMICs, and gives the same average for low-income and lower-middle-income countries. Using it as the second factor would amount to assuming that nearly nine in ten strokes are admitted in low-income countries.
- NCD Countdown's treatment of acute myocardial infarction (population in need equals all incident cases; baseline coverage equals PURE availability times affordability) is the closest structural template for a hospital-based acute intervention in a recent Lancet global analysis, and it too has no separate reach term.
- The national models that begin from a population-based incidence register (NEMESIS, South London, ARCOS) are the only precedents where the non-hospitalised strokes are in the starting cohort; all are from high-income settings with admission above 85%.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that estimates deaths or disability averted globally by universal stroke-unit access. One web search and several PubMed searches returned only the WHO-CHOICE entries, the national models above and narrative reviews.
- No cost-effectiveness or impact model of stroke-unit care was found for India, Thailand, South Africa, Nigeria or Iran; PubMed returned thrombolysis or thrombectomy evaluations for China, Iran and Brazil, a tenecteplase cost-utility analysis for Iran (PMID 36420792, title only) and unrelated studies.
- Could not verify: the WHO-CHOICE cardiovascular technical brief behind CV7 and CV4a (the annex cites "separate technical briefs" without a link; a WHO-domain search returned only the annex and the diet brief); the OneHealth Tool or Spectrum NCD impact-module manual (not located); Bertram supplementary files 2 to 4 (not opened); country-level utilisation values in Kruk (the appendix tabulates only income-group averages); country-level baseline coverage values in the NCD Countdown appendix (method read, values not extracted); the Lozano appendix describing how the stroke mortality-to-incidence ratio is scaled (downloaded, not read); full texts of Gilligan 2005, Moodie 2004, Saka 2009, Te Ao 2012, Hankey and Warlow 1999 and Langhorne 2012 (abstracts only, so their hospitalisation and baseline-coverage inputs are not documented here); the WSO-Lancet Commission appendix.

## 3. Capacity and coverage datasets: stroke-unit availability and the share of stroke patients treated in stroke units, by country and year

### Takeaway
Counts or yes/no availability of stroke units exist for most regions, but the share of patients treated in a stroke unit, by country and year, is published systematically only for Europe (the SAP-E Stroke Service Tracker, annual since 2021) and in national audits of a few high-income countries, almost always with hospitalised or registry patients as the denominator. The global sources (WSO-WHO survey, WHO NCD Country Capacity Survey, INTERSTROKE, RES-Q) are hospital samples or ministry self-reports and cannot be read as national coverage.

### Inferences
- For the third factor, the only multi-country, multi-year series of the share of patients treated in a stroke unit is the SAP-E tracker, and for 2023 a third of reporting nations (16 of 47) gave no figure. Outside Europe the realistic inputs are counts of units (Africa, South-East Asia, Latin America), hospital-sample proportions (INTERSTROKE, RES-Q, WSO-WHO) and the WHO binary indicator. A global baseline will have to be modelled from these, and the Global Stroke Statistics 2019 compilation (35 countries with a proportion) is the obvious seed table to obtain.
- The WHO Country Capacity Survey item ("reaches 50% or more of patients in need") is the only indicator available for nearly every country and for five rounds (2015, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2023), but it changed form after 2015 (share of facilities, then share of patients in need), is a ministry self-report, and moves implausibly between rounds for low-income countries (about a third in 2019, 52% in 2023, above lower-middle-income countries at 44%). I would treat it as a covariate with a wide error, not as a coverage estimate.
- Hospital-sample figures overstate national availability in LMICs by construction, a point both INTERSTROKE and the WSO-WHO survey make themselves. INTERSTROKE's lower-middle and low-income stratum (61% of patients in hospitals with a stroke unit) is higher than its upper-middle stratum (23%), which looks like site selection and not a gradient to extrapolate.
- The tracker's 2024 form asks for national incident totals and registry admissions separately and computes "coverage" from them. If those fields are released, the ratio is a direct, country-by-year estimate of the registered or hospitalised share of incident stroke in Europe, which is the second factor.

### Inferences
- Share of all first-ever strokes admitted to a stroke unit in South London, by my arithmetic from Table 2: 460 of 620, or 74%, in 2007-09 (equivalently 0.947 times 0.784), and 141 of 907, or 16%, in 1995-97. The 2007-09 figure is a slight underestimate because 3 admitted patients lack stroke-unit data, and both ignore the roughly 20% of strokes the register is estimated to miss, who are more likely to be non-hospitalised.
- The same table gives a clean precedent for separating the two factors: the admitted share rose from 82% to 95% over 15 years while the stroke-unit share among the admitted rose from 19% to 78%, so nearly all of the gain in population coverage came from the second term.
- If one reads the tracker totals as a registered share, 1,460,360 of 1,802,560 is 81% of estimated incident strokes in the WHO European region. The comparison is loose: the numerator counts first and recurrent strokes in 47 reporting nations, the denominator is incident stroke in 53 countries from a different study, and the authors attribute the gap mainly to five non-reporting countries.
- In the South London data non-admission is concentrated in milder strokes and is close to nil for haemorrhage and for patients with impaired consciousness. Where that pattern holds, the non-admitted have a lower baseline risk than the trial populations, so excluding them from the eligible base loses less benefit than their share suggests. The reverse is likely where the main route to non-admission is death before arrival or inability to pay, which is the situation the African delay data hint at; I found no numbers to settle it.

- Could not verify: ARCOS admission and stroke-unit shares from the primary papers (quoted here only as cited by Addo et al.); the Dijon, Finnish (PERFECT), Dutch and Scottish hospitalisation rates behind the SAFE list; Riksstroke's current stroke-unit share; whether the 2024 tracker "coverage" fields are published.

## 5. Definitional traps flagged by these sources

### Takeaway
The same phrase, "share of stroke patients treated in a stroke unit", is reported against at least four denominators and several definitions of a stroke unit, and the sources that have looked at this say cross-country comparison is unreliable without fixing both. For the second factor the main traps are first-ever versus all strokes, admission versus any facility contact, in-hospital strokes, and registries that record only patients who reached a stroke unit.

### Cited Findings
- Denominator, all strokes versus hospitalised: "some studies use only hospitalised stroke patients as their denominator (national audits), while others give the proportion of all stroke patients in a certain area (e.g. population-based registers). In this case, the proportion of stroke unit care is highly dependent on stroke hospitalisation rates". — [SAFE, The Burden of Stroke in Europe, 2017](https://www.safestroke.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/The-Burden-Of-Stroke-In-Europe-Report-Main-Document_ENG_All-references.pdf)
- Denominator, five variants seen in audits: "'all stroke patients', 'all ischaemic stroke patients', 'hospitalised stroke patients', 'stroke unit patients' and others, making reliable comparisons impossible. Furthermore, using hospitalised stroke patients as the denominator can be misleading, since hospitalisation rates for stroke vary across Europe." — [Aguiar de Sousa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/2396987318786023)
- The European target has shifted wording: 2018 "90% or more of all patients with stroke"; 2026 "90% or more of patients with acute stroke", monitored as "Percentage admitted to stroke unit care" among registry patients, while the same document states "the denominator should be all strokes, independent of the site of admission" and reports progress "for at least 75% of hospitalised stroke patients". — [SAP-E 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2396987318808719); [SAP-E mid-term review 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/esj/aakaf026)
- Registry scope can bias the measured share: "Mortality as reported by the SST may also be artificially low or high if registration by a stroke registry is reserved to stroke units and admission to stroke unit is not mandatory for all stroke patients as first level of care ... Low mortality may result from admitting patients with no options of acute interventions to less specialised institutions, such as frail elderly patients and severely affected patients". — [Christensen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/esj/aakag008)
- First-ever versus all strokes: the tracker counts "strokes [first and recurrent]"; the European surveys use GBD "annual incident ischaemic strokes" (2019 paper) and "annual incident first-ever ischaemic strokes" (2023 update); the South London register records "first ever strokes"; among the trials, Athens 1995 "Excluded TIA or recurrent stroke" while Beijing 2004 took "first or recurrent stroke". — [Christensen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/esj/aakag008); [Aguiar de Sousa et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/23969873231186042); [Addo et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d744); [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4)
- Incidence denominators are themselves uncertain: "Estimated stroke incidence rates of the 2015 Global Burden of Disease Report ... might eventually be too high for some countries, while for other countries, rates might be too low"; for Russia, "According to the local experts, the incidence of ischemic stroke is overestimated." — [Aguiar de Sousa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/2396987318786023)
- Subtype scope: the Cochrane review excludes "subarachnoid haemorrhage and subdural haematoma"; the South London register excludes subarachnoid haemorrhage; the tracker's stroke-unit count covers "ischaemic + ICH" while its incidence page asks separately for "stroke, TIA, and SAH". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4); [Addo et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d744); [SST Guide 2024](https://actionplan.eso-stroke.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/SAP-E-Stroke-Services-Tracker-Guide-for-NCs_2024.pdf)
- Admission versus any hospital or facility contact: AuSCR counts patients "admitted, or presenting to" emergency departments and reports per episode (19,831 episodes for 17,970 patients); Kruk's utilisation measure is "Sought care at in- or outpatient facility"; the WHO survey asks whether care "reaches 50% or more patients in need" in "the public health system". — [AuSCR 2024](https://auscr.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/auscr-annual-report-2024-and-2025-status-update_final18dec25.pdf); [Kruk et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31668-4); [WHO 2021 report](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/370423)
- Strokes with onset in hospital: 11.7% of hospital-treated first-ever strokes in South London; in AuSCR such patients received stroke-unit care in 51% of episodes against 81% for community-onset strokes. — [Addo et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d744); [AuSCR 2024](https://auscr.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/auscr-annual-report-2024-and-2025-status-update_final18dec25.pdf)
- "Treated in a stroke unit" has at least four operational forms: care in a stroke unit "at any point" (tracker); admitted "within 24 h of arrival" (SAP-E KPI 6b); "directly admitted to a stroke unit within 4 hours" and "at least 90% of their hospital stay" (SSNAP, 46.5% and 74.0% in the same year); "more than 50% of hospital admission spent in a stroke unit" (South London, EROS). The SAFE report notes that "some studies only looked at subsets of stroke patients, e.g. those admitted to a stroke unit within a certain time of hospital arrival or patients spending a certain proportion of their hospital stay in a stroke unit". — [SST Guide 2024](https://actionplan.eso-stroke.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/SAP-E-Stroke-Services-Tracker-Guide-for-NCs_2024.pdf); [SSNAP 2025](https://www.hqip.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Ref-650-SSNAP-State-of-the-Nation-2025-FINAL.pdf); [Addo et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d744); [SAFE 2017](https://www.safestroke.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/The-Burden-Of-Stroke-In-Europe-Report-Main-Document_ENG_All-references.pdf)
- What counts as a stroke unit varies: "definitions of what is called a 'stroke unit' are not always the same and significant differences in standards of care can be found" (SAFE); "Given the lack of a uniform definition of SUs, some differences in SU rates are likely to be related to differences in definitions" (European survey, which used local definitions); "no complete information on definitions of stroke units and comprehensive stroke centres in these countries is available" (SAP-E 2026). — [SAFE 2017](https://www.safestroke.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/The-Burden-Of-Stroke-In-Europe-Report-Main-Document_ENG_All-references.pdf); [Aguiar de Sousa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/2396987318786023); [SAP-E mid-term review 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/esj/aakaf026)
- Trial definition against survey definitions: the Cochrane core is "(1) multi-disciplinary staffing ... and (2) co-ordinated multi-disciplinary team care incorporating meetings at least once per week"; the Commission lists "three types of stroke units: acute stroke units with monitored beds for patients in the first 72 h ... rehabilitation stroke units for stabilised patients 48-72 h after symptoms onset; and comprehensive stroke units"; the African survey deliberately dropped thrombolysis from its definition ("mandating thrombolysis services to name a dedicated portion in a hospital a stroke unit was skipped"); RES-Q reports a combined "SU/ICU" category; Germany distinguishes 463 "stroke-ready hospitals" from 349 "certified stroke units". — [Cochrane CD000197.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000197.pub4); [Feigin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(23)00277-6); [Roushdy et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10072-024-07982-y); [Mikulik et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/17474930251364082); [Lancet Reg Health Eur 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2026.101604)
- Stroke unit versus stroke centre versus rehabilitation unit: SAP-E defines a stroke unit as "A dedicated geographically clearly defined area or ward in a hospital" and a stroke centre as "A hospital infrastructure and related processes of care that provide the full pathway of stroke unit care"; it treats the "stroke rehabilitation unit" as "an evidence-based alternative for continuing in-patient treatment"; its authors insist that a narrow "ICU-like" reading "is not consistent with the evidence at hand, which extends to mixed acute and rehabilitative stroke units and specialised rehabilitative stroke units". The Commission's Latin American count is of "stroke centres, all of which provide thrombolysis", and in Brazil only "46% of hospitals" classed as stroke centres "had stroke units". — [SAP-E 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2396987318808719); [Response letter 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/esj/aakag059); [Feigin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(23)00277-6); [Cerebrovasc Dis 2026](https://doi.org/10.1159/000546276)
- Availability is not access: INTERSTROKE's exposure is "Admission to a hospital with a stroke unit", and it separately records whether the unit has "Capacity to look after >50% of patients" (89% of patients in high-income hospitals, 22% upper-middle, 34% lower-middle or low). The Commission notes that "even in HICs the number of beds available in stroke units is often insufficient to provide adequate acute care for all patients with stroke". — [Langhorne et al. 2018, accepted manuscript](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/162861/1/162861.pdf); [Feigin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(23)00277-6)
- Percent of hospitals versus percent of countries: the WSO-WHO survey abstract says "Stroke units were present in 91% of high-income countries in contrast to 18% of low-income countries", whereas the underlying counts are hospitals ("117 (91%) of 129 hospitals in HICs ... three (18%) of 17 hospitals in low-income countries"). — [Owolabi et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/17474930211019568); [Feigin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(23)00277-6)
- Self-report and question drift in the WHO survey: 2015 asked for the "percentage of public sector health facilities" with provision "for care of acute stroke and rehabilitation" (one combined item); from 2017 acute care and rehabilitation are separate and "generally available" means "reaches 50% or more patients in need"; in 2021 rehabilitation was split into inpatient and outpatient, and in 2023 into primary, secondary and tertiary levels. "Non-positive responses (i.e. 'No', 'Don't know', and items left unanswered) were treated equally." — [2015 report](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/246223); [2021 report](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/370423); [2023 report](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/381204)
- Timing of the data: "due to the significant increase in stroke unit care over recent years, figures depend on when the data were collected". — [SAFE 2017](https://www.safestroke.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/The-Burden-Of-Stroke-In-Europe-Report-Main-Document_ENG_All-references.pdf)
- Within-country variation can exceed between-country variation: "within-country variations might be as large, or even larger than variations between national averages"; the European survey's counts rest "on the assumption that existing SUs are equally distributed within countries"; in Nigeria "available facilities [are] located mainly in major cities with tertiary hospitals". — [SAFE 2017](https://www.safestroke.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/The-Burden-Of-Stroke-In-Europe-Report-Main-Document_ENG_All-references.pdf); [Aguiar de Sousa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/2396987318786023); [Feigin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(23)00277-6)
- Comparator trap in the cost-effectiveness precedents: WHO-CHOICE evaluates against "a hypothetical 'null' scenario, where the impacts of all currently implemented interventions are removed", so its results are not incremental to current coverage; NCD Countdown instead models change from baseline coverage. — [WHO technical annex 2022](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/ncds/mnd/2022-app3-technical-annex-v26jan2023.pdf); [Watkins et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(21)02347-3)

### Inferences
- A definition that fits both the trials and the audit data is "incident strokes (ischaemic or intracerebral haemorrhage, first-ever or recurrent as the GBD forecast defines them) admitted as inpatients to any hospital in the acute or early subacute phase". Baseline coverage would then be "of those, the share cared for in a unit meeting the Cochrane core definition", which is the denominator national audits and the SAP-E tracker already use. The product of the two is the "all strokes" coverage that SAP-E says should be the standard and that only population registers report.
- If the GBD forecast counts first-ever strokes only, recurrent strokes admitted to hospital are eligible in the trials' sense but absent from the base; the audit shares (SSNAP, AuSCR, tracker) include them. The team should state which it is using, because recurrent events are a substantial share of admissions in registry data.
- Because "stroke unit" in LMIC surveys often means a dedicated area without the multidisciplinary staffing the trials tested (INTERSTROKE: about a fifth of patients outside high-income countries were in a hospital whose unit met all key characteristics), counting units as coverage will overstate baseline organised care; the Cochrane review's finding that mobile teams did not show benefit argues against crediting anything short of a geographically defined ward with a coordinated team.

### Gaps
