## Key question 1: Which databases report hospital discharges for cerebrovascular disease (I60-I69) or acute stroke (I60-I64) by country and year, for how many countries and years, and in what units?

### Takeaway
Three multi-country series exist (OECD, 46 countries, 1980 to 2025; Eurostat, 36 countries, 1995 to 2021 plus a new 2022 to 2023 series; WHO Europe HFA-DB, 1970 to 2023), all of which count discharges (episodes, including deaths and inter-hospital transfers) for the broad I60-I69 group, not persons and not acute stroke. Outside the OECD and Europe there is no multi-country cause-specific series; what exists is national, usually public-sector-only or tertiary-only, and GBD itself needs an all-cause "envelope" to scale 12 of these sources and discards stroke admissions from 14 locations as implausibly low.

### Inferences
- The multi-country series measure I60-I69 episodes. None reports acute stroke (I60-I64 or I61, I63, I64) separately, and none reports persons. I60-I69 includes I65-I66 (occlusion without infarction), I67-I68 (other cerebrovascular disease) and I69 (sequelae), which is where long-stay, rehabilitation and "chronic cerebrovascular insufficiency" admissions land. The more than 5-fold spread among countries with complete national reporting (152 per 100,000 in Canada to 823 in Bulgaria in 2024; 20-fold if Argentina and Mexico at 41 to 44 are included), and Hungary's fall from 997 to 462 per 100,000 between 2010 and 2024, are too large to be incidence and are better read as coding, coverage and admission-practice differences.
- The main reason each source could mislead as a numerator for "incident strokes reaching hospital": OECD and Eurostat, broad code group and episodes rather than persons; WHO HFA-DB, the same plus undocumented breaks back to 1970; OECD 30-day mortality, a quality indicator whose denominators are not public; Ireland, Portugal, Cyprus, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, Ukraine, Argentina, Brazil and probably Mexico and Costa Rica, public sector only; Colombia, reporting so incomplete (1 to 2 per 100,000) as to be unusable; China HQMS, tertiary-weighted, hospital panel changing from 1,599 to 7,536, and admissions that include TIA and repeat stays; India NSS, self-report with recall and proxy-respondent error.
- Realistic country-year coverage for an "admissions" numerator is about 46 countries, almost all high-income, mostly from 2000 on. In the OECD series only 7 countries start by 1990 (Austria, Canada, Spain, Greece, Netherlands, Norway, United States) and 18 by 1999, and for the roughly 150 remaining countries there is nothing comparable.

## Key question 2: What do validation studies say about administrative stroke admissions versus population-based registry counts, and what fraction of events is hospitalised under the "hospitalised events plus out-of-hospital stroke deaths" method?

### Takeaway
Against a registry or chart review, acute stroke discharge codes typically have sensitivity near 80% and positive predictive value near 70 to 90%, but raw discharge counts run about 2 to 3 times the number of incident events, and population-based studies in high-income settings still find that 10 to 37% of incident strokes are never admitted. The linked-data method has been applied nationally in Norway, Sweden, Scotland, England, New Zealand and (unlinked) Australia, and in parts of Canada and Australia; only Norway publishes the split, with out-of-hospital deaths at 7.6% of incident cases for 2001 to 2021, and that method by construction misses non-fatal strokes that are never admitted.

### Cited Findings

**Systematic reviews of coding accuracy**
- 77 validation studies (USA 31, Europe 26, Canada 13, Asia 4, Australia 2): for the broad group ICD-9 430-438 or ICD-10 I60-I69, sensitivity was at least 82% in 7 of 14 studies (range 32 to 100%) and PPV at least 81% in 7 of 14 (range 43 to 97%); "the PPV specifically for acute stroke was ≤ 68%" in 12 of 21 studies (range 28 to 98%). By subtype, PPV was at least 93% for subarachnoid haemorrhage, 89% for intracerebral haemorrhage and 82% for ischaemic stroke in half or more of studies, while ischaemic stroke sensitivity ranged from 2 to 80%. The authors recommend excluding ICD-10 I62 and I67-I69 and keeping I64, and conclude "acute stroke must be defined using more specific codes". Only 12 studies used a registry or clinical database as reference; 35 used chart review. [P] — McCormick et al., PLoS One 2015 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0135834)
- 39 studies: PPVs "ranged from 6–97%"; stroke-specific codes 68 to 90%, broad cerebrovascular codes 31 to 80%; "Sensitivities for identifying stroke were around 80% or more" from hospital data alone or with death certificates. Only six studies used a population-based reference, four of them Scandinavian. The authors note that "up to half of stroke patients are not admitted to hospital in the UK". [P] — Woodfield et al., PLoS One 2015 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0140533)

**Country studies against a population-based or clinical reference**
- England (OXVASC, 2002 to 2017): "Of 3011 strokes, 1883 (62.5%) were hospitalised, 1044 (34.7%) were managed in outpatient clinics or the community"; "Of 2254 OXVASC incident strokes, 833 (37.0%) were not hospitalised." Among hospitalised strokes, "1341 (71.2%) were correctly identified by coding" (I60-I61, I63-I64 as primary diagnosis); sensitivity was 75.7% for those admitted for suspected stroke and 39.8% for strokes during another admission. PPV was 83.2% for specific codes against 69.2% for all codes I60-I68. Of false positives with non-specific codes, 52.1% were "elective admissions for rehabilitation, investigations or procedures for prior stroke" and 41.0% non-stroke diagnoses. In 2014 to 2017, 229 of 462 incident strokes (49.6%) would have been missed by hospital coding alone. [P] — Li et al., Eur Stroke J 2020 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/2396987319881017)
- France (Dijon registry against the hospital discharge database, 2004 to 2008): 811 registry strokes, 186 missed; 903 coded, 278 false positive; "overall sensitivity and PPV ... were, respectively, 77.1 % ... and 69.2 %". [A] — Aboa-Eboulé et al., J Neurol 2013 (PMID 23076827) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-012-6686-0)
- Sweden (patient plus cause-of-death registers against Northern Sweden MONICA): after restriction to first-ever strokes, PPV 94% and sensitivity 92%; for attacks (first and recurrent), PPV 85% and sensitivity 91%; both lower among fatal cases. [A] — Köster et al., Neuroepidemiology 2013 (PMID 23364278) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1159/000345953)
- Sweden (Örebro community register, 1999 to 2000): of 377 first-ever strokes, "Forty-four of these (12%) were missing in the IPR/CDR. Non-hospitalized patients were less likely to be registered"; 30 patients (8%) in the registers were misclassified. [A] — Appelros and Terént, Acta Neurol Scand 2011 (PMID 21361878) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0404.2010.01402.x)
- Sweden (Lund, 2015 to 2016): of 400 first-ever strokes from overlapping sources, "Riksstroke-Lund detected 328 (82%) patients, whereas LSR detected 363 (91%)"; undetected patients had 28-day case fatality of 44% against 9%; patients found only in primary care more often lived in care facilities. [A] — Aked et al., Acta Neurol Scand 2020 (PMID 31664726) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/ane.13187)
- Sweden, Riksstroke coverage: "Of all diagnosed first-ever strokes reported to at least one of the registers in 2024, 95 percent were found in the patient register and 86 percent in Riksstroke" (my translation of "återfanns 95 procent i patientregistret och 86 procent i Riksstroke"). [P] — [Socialstyrelsen quality declaration 2025](https://www.socialstyrelsen.se/contentassets/8984a80057994a3090c3d8b62cc23ca9/2025-11-9883-kvalitetsdeklaration.pdf)
- Sweden, Riksstroke: "The estimated coverage rate of Riksstroke was stable at about 90% of admitted acute stroke cases in Sweden" for 2010 to 2019. [P] — Drescher et al., Neuroepidemiology 2022 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1159/000527373)
- Finland (hospital discharge and cause-of-death registers against FINSTROKE, 1993 to 1998): "The sensitivity for all first stroke events was 85%, for fatal strokes 86% and for non-fatal strokes 85%. The positive predictive values for all first strokes was 86%". [A] — Tolonen et al., Eur J Cardiovasc Prev Rehabil 2007 (PMID 17568236) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.hjr.0000239466.26132.f2)
- Norway (medical record review of 5,192 admissions in 2012): Norwegian Patient Register main plus secondary diagnoses, sensitivity 96.8% and PPV 79.7%; main diagnosis only, sensitivity 86.1% and PPV 93.5%; Norwegian Stroke Register sensitivity 88.1%. [A] — Varmdal et al., Scand J Public Health 2016 (PMID 26660300) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1403494815621641)
- Denmark: the National Patient Registry had sensitivity 79% and PPV 79% in one sample, and sensitivity 58% in a second (neurology wards, 2010). [A] — Wildenschild et al., Clin Epidemiol 2014 (PMID 24399886) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2147/clep.s50449)
- Canada (Ontario Stroke Registry reference, 2006 to 2008): "Inpatient administrative data had an overall sensitivity of 82.2% ... and a PPV of 68.8% ... for the diagnosis of stroke"; emergency department data 56.8% and 59.1%. [A] — Hall et al., Can J Neurol Sci 2016 (PMID 27426016) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/cjn.2016.278)
- New Zealand (national REGIONS Care study against linked administrative data, 2018): sensitivity 83%, PPV 77%, with 300 false negatives and 414 false positives among about 1,700 to 1,800 cases. [A] — Corbin et al., Lancet Reg Health West Pac 2026 (PMID 41550612) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanwpc.2025.101768)
- Taiwan (NHI claims against the Taiwan Stroke Registry, one centre): ICD-9 433/434, PPV 88.4% and sensitivity 97.3%. [A] — Hsieh et al., J Formos Med Assoc 2015 (PMID 24140108) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfma.2013.09.009). With ICD-10-CM I63 in any position, PPV 92.7% and sensitivity 99.4%; as primary diagnosis only, PPV 99.8% with more than 12% lower sensitivity. [A] — Hsieh et al., Clin Epidemiol 2020 (PMID 33061648) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2147/CLEP.S273853)
- Korea (claims algorithm against record review of 1,086 stroke cases in 14 hospitals): sensitivity 94.3%, specificity 88.6%; the authors list "our inability to include non-hospitalized incident cases, including pre-hospitalization fatalities" as a limitation. [P] — Kim et al., Osong Public Health Res Perspect 2024 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.24171/j.phrp.2023.0248)

**Admissions relative to events (inflation)**
- United States, 1996: 2,082,005 discharges carried a cerebrovascular diagnosis; after weighting each first-listed code by its PPV (code 434, 82%; code 433, 15%; code 438, 1%) the estimate was about 712,000 hospital strokes, and adding 10% for non-hospital strokes (from two population studies giving 5% and 15%) gave "783,000 first-ever or recurrent strokes". [P] — Williams, BMC Neurol 2001 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2377-1-2)
- Scotland, 2023/24: the age-sex standardised discharge rate for stroke (I61, I63, I64) was 473.4 per 100,000 (up from 422.2 in 2014/15), while the age-sex standardised incidence rate for stroke was 174 per 100,000. [P] — [Scottish Stroke Statistics report](https://publichealthscotland.scot/media/31155/2025-01-28-stroke-report_final.pdf)
- Canada, 2017-18: "108,707 stroke events resulting in hospital or emergency department presentation ... made up of 54,357 events resulting in hospital admission and 54,350 events resulting in only emergency department presentation", after linking records "into episodes of care to account for readmissions and interfacility transfers". [A] — Holodinsky et al., Can J Neurol Sci 2023 (PMID 36536997) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/cjn.2022.338). The companion analysis shows that these "stroke events" include TIA (31,020 of 81,781 events outside Quebec) and states "our study did not include individuals with stroke who did not present for care". [P] — Wan et al., Can J Neurol Sci 2024 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/cjn.2023.290)
- GBD 2023 crosswalks, expressed as the "logit difference between alternative and reference definitions" with reference "first-ever, subtype-specific stroke, which included subjects who did not survive to hospital admission": inpatient administrative data intercepts 0.44 (factor 1.55) for ischaemic stroke, 0.94 (2.56) for intracerebral haemorrhage and 1.24 (3.46) for subarachnoid haemorrhage; US claims 0.89 (2.43), 1.63 (5.10) and 2.25 (9.47). Studies "which included only stroke cases which survived to hospital admission" have intercepts −0.21 (factor 0.81, 95% CI of beta −0.33 to −0.09) for ischaemic stroke, −0.08 (0.93, −0.29 to 0.13) for intracerebral haemorrhage, and −0.14 (0.87, −0.28 to −0.01) for subarachnoid haemorrhage, with further age-spline and sex terms. [P] — [GBD 2023 appendix, stroke Tables 4a-4c, p.769-784](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2825%2901637-X)
- In the WHO MONICA stroke populations, "combined stroke attack rates for first and recurrent events were approximately 20% higher than incidence rates in most populations". [A] — Thorvaldsen et al., Stroke 1995 (PMID 7886707) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.str.26.3.361)

**The "hospitalised events plus out-of-hospital deaths" method**
- Norway, 2001 to 2021 (Norwegian Patient Registry and Cause of Death Registry; I61, I63 except I63.6, I64; incident case is "an inpatient hospitalization with stroke as the main or secondary diagnosis" or "out‐of‐hospital death with stroke as the underlying cause", "with no previous hospitalization for stroke in the past 7 years"): "we identified 229 857 incident total stroke cases"; "17 469 (7.6%) were out‐of‐hospital deaths (66.1% women)"; "212 388 (92.4%) were inpatient hospitalizations"; "the proportion with incident out‐of‐hospital stroke deaths decreased from 10.5% to 4.3%". Of out-of-hospital deaths, 81.6% were unspecified stroke. [P] — Kvalvaag et al., J Am Heart Assoc 2025 (PMID 41378456) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.125.041029)
- Norway, 2010 to 2015 (I61, I63, I64, primary diagnosis; individuals counted once per year, first-ever not identifiable): 53,431 individuals hospitalised and "6,315 additional individuals registered as dead due to stroke"; individuals with stroke per 100,000 person-years "dropped from 239 to 195 (208 to 177 excluding immediate deaths)". For deaths with no preceding hospitalisation "clinical judgement plays a greater role, with comparatively lower validity"; outpatient visits were excluded as "uninformative as to stroke incidence". [P] — Rand et al., BMC Health Serv Res 2019 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-019-4538-7)
- Sweden: the incidence date is the inpatient admission date, and "in cases where the patient died without having received hospital care the incidence date is estimated from the date of death in the cause-of-death register" (my translation); all events from day 0 to day 27 belong to one case. Of 2024 cases about 11% died the same day, about 22% within 28 days and 32% within 365 days. The agency states that "virtually all cases of stroke are captured". The split between hospitalised cases and deaths without hospital care is not published in the report. [P] — [Socialstyrelsen, Statistik om stroke 2024](https://www.socialstyrelsen.se/contentassets/8984a80057994a3090c3d8b62cc23ca9/2025-11-9883.pdf) and [quality declaration](https://www.socialstyrelsen.se/contentassets/8984a80057994a3090c3d8b62cc23ca9/2025-11-9883-kvalitetsdeklaration.pdf)
- Scotland: "an incident case is defined as the first admission to hospital (or death without a hospital admission). A first admission is defined as an admission where there has been no admission for the same condition in the previous 10 years." The publication adds: "First hospital admission is a reasonable measure of incidence for CVD since most cases are treated in hospital." Incident stroke cases numbered 9,792 in 2023/24 (provisional) and 9,281 in 2019/20; the workbook does not separate hospital cases from deaths. [P] — [Scottish Stroke Statistics report](https://publichealthscotland.scot/media/31155/2025-01-28-stroke-report_final.pdf) and [Table 3 incidence workbook](https://publichealthscotland.scot/media/31152/table3_incidence_stroke_2025_01_28.xlsx)
- England, 2001 to 2010: a stroke event is "hospital admission for stroke, or a death with stroke as the underlying cause without a corresponding hospital admission for stroke in the preceding 30 days", codes I61-I64, emergency admissions and transfers only, excluding live discharges after one day or less. There were 947,497 events including 337,085 deaths in 795,869 people; the split by admission status is not reported. The authors note the method "might have missed cases of stroke that were treated wholly without hospital admission". [P] — Seminog et al., BMJ 2019 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l1778)
- Australia: AIHW derives stroke events "using proxy measures, based on unlinked, routinely available hospitalisation data ... and deaths data"; the algorithms "account for repeat admissions for one event and possible duplicates across the two datasets, but cannot differentiate between recurrent and new events". A linked-data check found that "stroke events estimated using the unlinked data were similar to estimates from linked data", and that almost 29,000 Australians had a first stroke in 2018. [S] — [AIHW technical report page](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/heart-stroke-vascular-diseases/incidence-of-stroke-acute-coronary-syndrome/contents/technical-report)
- Australia (Western Australia, South Australia, Northern Territory; 2012 to 2015; 10-year lookback): 11,740 incident strokes at ages 20 to 84, 15.6% fatal, "including out-of-hospital deaths". [A] — Balabanski et al., Stroke 2023 (PMID 37325922) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1161/strokeaha.122.041975)
- Five jurisdictions (Western Australia, England, New Zealand, British Columbia, Ontario; 2002 to 2018) have been analysed together with "linked hospitalization and mortality data"; 2,248,242 stroke events. [A] — Nedkoff et al., Int J Epidemiol 2026 (PMID 42758057) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyag157)

### Inferences
- Computed here from the Norwegian counts: hospitalised cases were 92.4% of the routine-data incident total for 2001 to 2021, rising from 89.5% to 95.7% over the period; in the 2010 to 2015 analysis, deaths without hospitalisation were 6,315 of 59,746 individuals (10.6%), or 12.8% in 2010 and 9.0% in 2015. These are upper bounds on the true fraction of incident strokes admitted, because the denominator omits non-fatal strokes never admitted.
- How large that omission is, in the places that have measured it: 37% of incident strokes in Oxfordshire were not hospitalised; 12% of first-ever strokes in Örebro were in neither Swedish register; hospital-based registers in Lund missed 9 to 18%; the US national estimate assumed 10%. GBD's pooled crosswalk for hospital-only studies (0.81 for ischaemic stroke, 0.93 for intracerebral haemorrhage, 0.87 for subarachnoid haemorrhage at the intercept) says the same thing from the literature as a whole, and it carries no health-system covariate.
- Computed here: in Scotland the standardised stroke discharge rate is 2.7 times the standardised incidence rate (473.4 / 174); in Australia hospitalisations with stroke as principal diagnosis are about 1.8 times estimated stroke events (73,000 / 41,100) and about 2.5 times first-ever strokes (73,000 / 29,000, different years); in the United States in 1996 discharges with any cerebrovascular code were 2.9 times PPV-adjusted hospital strokes (2,082,005 / 712,000).
- What inflates admissions: recurrent events (attack rates about 20% above incidence), transfers counted as discharges (Eurostat and Scotland include them), readmissions and rehabilitation or elective episodes coded as stroke (half of OXVASC false positives), broad codes (I65-I69, and in the US the 433 and 438 series), TIA folded into "stroke" (Canada, Scotland's cerebrovascular series), and secondary-position diagnoses (US claims). What deflates them: non-admission of mild strokes and of deaths before arrival, strokes during another admission (sensitivity 40% in OXVASC), database coverage gaps (private sector, non-reporting hospitals), and omission from coding altogether (53% of OXVASC false negatives).
- The net of these is positive in every complete system examined, so an unadjusted "admissions ÷ incidence" ratio exceeds 1 and cannot be read as a fraction.

### Gaps
- Could not verify: the AIHW technical report's algorithm details and its counts of non-fatal hospitalised, fatal hospitalised and non-hospitalised fatal events. Tried WebFetch on www.aihw.gov.au and pp.aihw.gov.au, curl on the report pages and two PDFs (all HTTP 403), and web.archive.org (blocked by the fetch tool).
- No published split of hospitalised cases against deaths without hospitalisation was found for Sweden, Scotland, England, Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands, New Zealand or Canada. Sweden's same-day case fatality (11%) bounds it from above. The Scottish and Swedish agencies could produce it from data they already hold.
- Not found: an ARCOS (Auckland) comparison with the National Minimum Dataset, a Japanese validation of DPC stroke codes against a population registry, any validation in a low- or middle-income country against a population-based registry, and the Riksstroke annual report itself (I used the Socialstyrelsen declaration, which reports the same coverage comparison).
- The Greater Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky statement that out-of-hospital ascertainment accounts for "between 10% and 20%" of strokes comes from a search summary only; the retrieved abstract confirms the design, "Out-of-hospital events were ascertained by prospective screening of emergency department admission logs, review of coroners' cases, and monitoring all public health and hospital-based primary care clinics" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.str.0000129335.28301.f5)), but not the percentage.

## Key question 3: Has anyone published stroke admissions as a share of expected incident strokes for low- and middle-income countries?

### Takeaway
I found no published analysis that divides national stroke admissions by expected incident strokes for any low- or middle-income country; the nearest thing is a single Mexican municipality where hospital surveillance and a door-to-door survey were run side by side. When I form the ratio myself from retrieved numbers it ranges from 0.02 to 3.6, tracking database coverage and coding rather than access.

### Cited Findings

## Key question 4: Candidate covariates: definition, coverage, download location, forecasts to 2050, and evidence that each predicts hospital use for acute events

### Takeaway
Complete 1990 to 2023 country-year coverage exists only for modelled IHME covariates (HAQ Index, UHC index, lag-distributed income, education, in-facility delivery, skilled birth attendance) and for urbanisation; forecasts to 2050 were verified only for urbanisation (UN) and health spending by source including out-of-pocket (IHME), and none was found for HAQ. Direct evidence that any of these predicts the share of acute events reaching hospital is thin; the strongest precedent is that GBD itself uses HAQ in a logit model for the proportion of myocardial infarctions that reach care.

- Global travel-time maps: "just 8.9% of the global population (646 million people) cannot reach healthcare within one hour if they have access to motorized transport, and ... 43.3% (3.16 billion people) cannot reach a healthcare facility by foot within one hour". The authors say the maps "can help to estimate whether individuals will seek healthcare when it is needed". Available from the Malaria Atlas Project site and Google Earth Engine. [S] — [Google Earth Engine catalogue](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/projects_malariaatlasproject_assets_accessibility_accessibility_to_healthcare_2019_walking_only)
- Sub-Saharan Africa, 2015: 4,908 public hospitals in 48 countries; "287 282 013 (29·0%) people ... are located more than 2-h travel time from the nearest hospital", "ranging from less than 25% of the population within 2-h travel time of a public hospital in South Sudan to more than 90% in Nigeria, Kenya, Cape Verde, Swaziland, South Africa, Burundi, Comoros, São Tomé and Príncipe, and Zanzibar. Only 16 countries reached the international benchmark of more than 80%". [A] — Ouma et al., Lancet Glob Health 2018 (PMID 29396220) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X%2817%2930488-6)

This is a scenario for 2030, not a forecast of access. [A] — Falchetta et al., PNAS 2020 (PMID 33257557) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2009172117)
- Lancet Commission on Global Surgery indicator 1 is the share of the population able to reach, within two hours, a facility offering caesarean delivery, laparotomy and open fracture treatment, with a target of 80% per country by 2030; a 2026 systematic review found it "reported for 94 countries (39% meeting benchmark)" of 217. **Evidence that covariates predict use of hospital for acute events**
- HAQ as a predictor of reaching care, in GBD's own models: for myocardial infarction, a logit-space MR-BRT model of "the proportion of incident myocardial infarction that is diagnosed" with "covariates for age, sex, HAQ Ind[ex], and interaction term between age and HAQ Index, the IHD SEV scalar, and an intercept"; for injuries, "a reasonable cutoff for the HAQ Index (75 on a scale of 0–100) as the threshold at and above which 100% of injuries were treated", with the rest scaled "between 10% and 100% treated"; for inpatient data generally, a final adjustment "dividing the above estimates by a scalar ranging from 0 to 100" based on HAQ percentiles. [P] — [GBD 2023 appendix, p.29, p.724, p.759-760](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2825%2901637-X)
- Spending, beds and SDI as predictors of all-cause admissions: these are the three covariates in the GBD 2023 inpatient envelope model. [P] — [GBD 2023 appendix, p.21](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2825%2901637-X)
- Insurance and rural residence as predictors of stroke admission: the China Kadoorie Biobank gradients cited under question 3. [P] — [Levy et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X%2820%2930078-4)
- Distance: a meta-analysis of African studies found "increased distance from a health facility (AOR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.22–1.34)" associated with prehospital delay among stroke patients who reached hospital. [S] — [PMC12169519](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12169519/)
- Skilled birth attendance: met need for emergency obstetric care "was positively associated with skilled birth attendance" across 51 countries. [S] — [Holmer et al., BJOG 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/1471-0528.13230)

### Inferences
- The main reason each covariate could mislead as a predictor of the fraction of incident strokes admitted:
  - HAQ Index: built from mortality-to-incidence ratios produced by the same GBD models that supply the incidence forecast, so it is circular; it blends access with quality; and I found no forecast.
  - UHC effective coverage index and its stroke component: the stroke component is the stroke mortality-to-incidence ratio, which measures case fatality, so it moves with stroke-unit quality (the team's third and fourth factors) as much as with reaching hospital. Using it risks counting the same improvement twice.
  - Inpatient admissions per capita: closest in kind to the target, but it is a model output driven by spending, beds and SDI where data are absent, it stops in 2019, it mixes elective, obstetric and chronic admissions, and it is not publicly served for GBD 2023.
  - Hospital beds: measure capacity, not acute access. The countries at the top of the OECD cerebrovascular discharge table (Bulgaria, Latvia, Lithuania, Hungary) are ones I would expect to have high bed density, which suggests beds predict admissions for chronic "cerebrovascular" diagnoses as readily as admission of acute strokes; I did not test this. Series are gappy and have no forecast.
  - Physician density: counts all doctors wherever they work; sparse before 2000 for nurses.
  - Travel time: one epoch (2019), so it cannot give 1990 to 2050 without assumptions; proximity to "a hospital or clinic" is not proximity to a hospital that admits strokes; it captures geography but not cost or beliefs. It needs zonal statistics with population rasters to become a country number.
  - Urbanisation: complete and forecast to 2050, but the 2025 UN revision changes definition to the Degree of Urbanisation, and urban residence is a weak proxy where urban poor do not use hospitals.
  - Out-of-pocket share: nothing before 2000; a low share can mean good prepayment or simply little care bought.
  - EMS coverage: no country-year series; available surveys are one-off and partial.
  - In-facility delivery and skilled birth attendance: complete and well measured, but they track a programme-driven, predictable, young-adult event, and have risen fast under targeted financing while adult emergency care has not necessarily followed. I would expect them to overstate stroke access in countries with strong maternal programmes.
  - DHS care seeking: children only.
  - SDG 3.8.1 index: from 2000 only, and it contains beds and health-worker density already, so it adds little independent information.
- For the 2050 horizon the only inputs with verified forecasts are urban share and health spending by source. SDI is forecast inside IHME's work, and income and education projections exist elsewhere, but I did not verify a download. A model that needs to be projected is therefore safer if specified on income or SDI, health spending per capita and urban share than on HAQ.
- GBD's myocardial infarction model is a ready template: logit of the proportion reaching care, linear in a development or access index, with an age interaction because the old and the very severe are the ones who die before arrival.

### Gaps
- Could not verify whether stroke is among the 32 HAQ causes from a retrieved list (the abstract gives only the count); the circularity point stands either way through the pooled index.
- Could not verify the covariates used in the Moses et al. utilisation model or its results by income group; the Lancet full text returned HTTP 403.
- Could not verify a public download of IHME SDI, income or education forecasts to 2050, any forecast of beds, physicians, HAQ, UHC indices or facility delivery, or the UN WUP 2018 revision files.
- Could not verify the WHO road-safety indicator on the share of seriously injured patients transported by ambulance, or any central results database for the WHO Emergency Care System Assessment; a search found only scattered national assessments (for example Nepal in 2019).
- Could not verify whether the Lancet Commission's two-hour access indicator is carried in the World Development Indicators; a 2016 World Bank blog said it was "not populated with sufficient data", and I found no series code.
- No study was found that regresses hospital admission for stroke, or the hospitalised share of incident strokes, on country income or health-system variables, and none that relates travel time to population stroke admission rates in a low-income country.

## Key question 5: Analogous problems: how have others estimated the share of acute myocardial infarction, sepsis, injury or obstetric emergencies that reach hospital, and what functional form or covariates worked?

### Takeaway
Three working precedents exist: GBD's "prehospital myocardial infarction scalar" (a logit model in HAQ and age fitted to linked hospital and death data from five high-income sources), GBD's piecewise-linear HAQ rule for the share of injuries treated (10% at the bottom, 100% at HAQ 75 and above), and the UN "met need for emergency obstetric care" indicator, which divides treated complications by an assumed need and finds 21%, 32% and 99% in low-, middle- and high-income countries. All three lean on a development index because direct data in poor countries are missing, which is the position the stroke estimate is in.

### Cited Findings
- Myocardial infarction, GBD 2023: "We first estimated myocardial infarction incidence and prevalence of cases reaching health care (diagnosed myocardial infarction) using a DisMod-MR 2.1 model and separately modelled a scaling factor ... to capture prehospital myocardial infarction cases using a custom MR-BRT model." The input is the proportion of ischaemic heart disease deaths "associated with a diagnosis of myocardial infarction within 30 days of death", estimated from "individual-level and tabulated sources linking hospitalisation events to subsequent mortality records" (New Zealand, Friuli-Venezia Giulia, Sweden, England, Spain's REGICOR), "with age, sex, year, and the Healthcare Access and Quality (HAQ) Index as linear covariates". The scalar model "was run in logit space to constrain the proportion between 0 and 1" with "a spline with 4 internal knots on the interaction term between age and HAQ Index", and "We inverted the estimated proportions to produce the scaling factor". [P] — [GBD 2023 appendix, ischaemic heart disease, p.759-760](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2825%2901637-X)
- Myocardial infarction, England 2006 to 2010: of 135,950 deaths with acute myocardial infarction as underlying cause, "66 490 (49%) occurred in patients who were in hospital on the day of death or in the 28 days preceding death", and in a third of those the admission did not mention infarction. [A] — Asaria et al., Lancet Public Health 2017 (PMID 29253451) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667%2817%2930032-4)
- Coronary events, WHO MONICA 1985 to 1990 (29 populations, ages 35 to 64): median 28-day case fatality was 49% in men and 51% in women for all events against 22% and 27% for hospitalised events; "approximately two thirds of 28-day CHD deaths in men and women occurred before reaching the hospital". [A] — Chambless et al., Circulation 1997 (PMID 9403607) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.96.11.3849)
- Stroke, WHO MONICA: "A low ratio of MONICA stroke register to routine statistics of stroke mortality and a low proportion of nonfatal out-of-hospital events were the most common biases", such that 10 of 21 populations needed clarification before comparison. [A] — Asplund et al., Stroke 1995 (PMID 7886706) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.str.26.3.355)
- Injuries, GBD 2023: two severities are modelled, "injuries warranting inpatient care" being "injury cases of sufficient severity to require inpatient care if there are no restrictions in access to health care"; surveys that probe why care was not sought let GBD "include cases who cited financial or geographical barriers". The treated share uses the HAQ rule quoted under question 4. [P] — [GBD 2023 appendix, injuries, p.713 and p.724](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2825%2901637-X)
- Other GBD uses of the same device: for valve disease, "logit(y) = α+ β1 ∗haqi+ β2 ∗age+ β3 ∗severity" with "a prior that the proportion ... was zero where HAQ Index was equal to zero", fitted to data that "were all from relatively high-income geographies"; for congenital syphilis, "all countries at the 75th percentile HAQ Index and above treat 100%", scaled down below that. [P] — [GBD 2023 appendix, p.966 and p.1179](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2825%2901637-X)
- Sepsis: incidence is obtained by working backward from deaths, "Using 250 million hospital admissions and 7·82 million deaths from hospital data, representing 1310 location-years, we modelled case fatality rates by use of binomial logistic regression, applied to sepsis death estimates to estimate sepsis incidence". [A] — GBD 2021 Global Sepsis Collaborators, Lancet Glob Health 2025 (PMID 41135560) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X%2825%2900356-0)
- Emergency obstetric care: "Global met need for EmOC was 45% (IQR: 28-57%), with significant disparity between low- (21% [12-31%]), middle- (32% [15-56%]), and high-income countries (99% [99-99%])", from 62 studies in 51 countries; met need was inversely correlated with the maternal mortality ratio (r = −0.42). [S] — [Holmer et al., BJOG 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/1471-0528.13230)

### Inferences
- The obstetric indicator has the same structure as the stroke problem (treated cases in facilities over expected cases from a modelled incidence) and the same weakness: the answer is only as good as the assumed need and the completeness of facility counts. Its income gradient (21%, 32%, 99%) is the one cross-country number I found for an acute emergency. Whether stroke follows a gradient that steep is for the direct evidence (population-based studies, verbal autopsy) to say; nothing in these notes can test it.
- The myocardial infarction precedent suggests which data to seek for stroke: linked files giving the share of stroke deaths with no stroke admission in the preceding 28 or 30 days. Norway publishes it (7.6% of incident cases, falling to 4.3%); Sweden, Scotland, England, New Zealand, Western Australia, British Columbia and Ontario hold the data. Stroke differs from infarction in that sudden death is uncommon, so the fatal out-of-hospital share is small in rich countries and the larger unknown is the non-fatal stroke that is never admitted.
- Functional form: a logit in a development index with an age interaction, anchored at the top by the linked-data countries and the population-based studies, is the form with precedent. GBD's injury rule (a floor of 10% and a ceiling reached at HAQ 75) is cruder, but it has the merit of stating its assumption openly, and the stroke estimate will need a stated floor too because no administrative source can supply one.
- The sepsis approach offers a second, independent route worth noting for the team: divide stroke deaths occurring in hospital by an in-hospital case fatality to get hospitalised cases. That needs place-of-death data, which verbal autopsy and vital registration can supply and which other researchers on this project are covering.

### Gaps
- The GBD appendix figures showing the modelled proportion of myocardial infarctions reaching care by HAQ quintile, and the coefficients of that model, were not retrievable through the tool (figures and equation text were garbled), so I cannot give the proportion GBD assumes at low HAQ.
- The Holmer et al. numbers are from a search summary of the abstract; the full text and the country-level values were not retrieved. The sepsis 2020 paper's use of HAQ in its case-fatality model was not confirmed (the summary says only "in-hospital case-fatality was modelled for each location using linear regression").
- Nothing was found on the share of sepsis or injury cases reaching hospital measured directly across countries, and no stroke-specific regression of hospital admission on income or health-system variables.
