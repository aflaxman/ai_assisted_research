# Fraction of incident strokes reaching hospital in low- and middle-income countries: population-based incidence studies, survivor surveys and care-seeking studies (Asia, Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, Middle East and North Africa), about 1970 to 2025

Scope and method note. Abstracts and metadata were retrieved from PubMed (PubMed MCP tools and NCBI E-utilities), and open full texts from PubMed Central, Europe PMC and institutional repositories, all in this session (25 November 2026). Every percentage below is tied to text retrieved in this session, and each source carries its PMID and DOI link. Three evidence grades are used throughout: **P-FT** (primary study, full text retrieved), **P-AB** (primary study, abstract only), **S** (figure quoted in another paper that I retrieved, original not retrieved). Rows marked **PROXY** do not measure hospitalisation at all, and say what they measure instead. Arithmetic that I did myself is labelled "my calculation". Publisher sites for *Stroke*, *The Lancet* and Karger journals returned HTTP 403 or a paywall, so several of the best-known studies could only be read as abstracts; these are listed under "Could not verify" in the Gaps of the first key question.

## Key question 1. What percentage of incident strokes were hospitalised in each LMIC population-based study?

### Takeaway
Verified values run from 3 to 7% (rural low-income Tianjin, China, 1990s) to 93 to 97% (Martinique 1998-99, Matão, Brazil 2003-04), with urban Indian registries of the mid-2000s at 67 to 69% and the other Latin American registries at 71 to 86%. For sub-Saharan Africa and for the Middle East and North Africa I found no study that reports the share of all incident strokes admitted; the African evidence that exists (Tanzania and Sierra Leone, both leaning on verbal autopsy) suggests that fewer than half of strokes there are seen in hospital, though neither study measures that directly, and the Middle Eastern evidence is absent rather than reassuring.

### Inferences
- Levels by region, reading the tables with their grades in mind. **Latin America and the Caribbean**: five registry estimates between 1998 and 2016 give 71%, 84%, 86%, 93.5% and 97% admitted, so a central value near 85% with a low outlier (Iquique 2000-02). The 1995 Joinville registry and the Durango hospital surveillance show that earlier or simpler designs did not look for non-admitted cases at all. **Urban India, mid-2000s**: 67% (Mumbai) and 69% (Kolkata, secondary). **Rural India**: 26% in rural West Bengal (secondary, 1990s or early 2000s), and 60% among survivors in tea-estate communities in 2024-25, which is an upper bound for all incident strokes because the dead are missing from a survivor survey. **Rural low-income China**: 7% in the 1990s, 16% in the 2000s, 54% in the 2010s. **Sub-Saharan Africa**: no direct estimate; the Tanzanian data bound the share seen alive by any surveillance (hospital or community) at 29% rural and 38% urban, and the Freetown data show about half of fatal strokes under age 70 with any formal care and one fifth dying in hospital. **Middle East and North Africa**: nothing verified.
- "Admitted" and "assessed" diverge a great deal, and the multiplication in the planned model needs the first. In Tianjin in 2010-2018, 90.5% of first-ever strokes had a CT scan but 54.2% were admitted; in Ludhiana city a third of registered cases came from imaging centres; in Ñuble 85.7% were admitted and 90% scanned. Where only CT proportions among survivors are available (Bangladesh 24% rural and about two-thirds nationally, Sri Lanka 58%, Cachar 48%), they are best read as a rough indicator of contact with a hospital-level facility, not of admission.
- Registry designs overstate the admitted share when case-finding itself runs through facilities. The Indian national programme says so directly, and the Lagos registry is the clearest example: 84.6% of its cases were hospitalised, but its crude incidence (25 per 100,000) is about a quarter of the crude incidence in the Tanzanian sites (95 and 108 per 100,000), which suggests that most strokes in Surulere never reached a participating facility. This comparison is mine and crosses countries, so it carries little weight on its own.
- Survivor surveys overstate the admitted share for a different reason: early deaths at home are excluded. The Gadchiroli and Faridabad death series (87% and 46% of stroke deaths at home) show how large that excluded group can be in India.
- A working reading for the second factor, offered as judgment and not as a finding: upper-middle-income Latin American settings 0.70 to 0.95 since 2000; urban India about 0.65 to 0.70 in the mid-2000s with no verified recent value; rural India somewhere between 0.25 and 0.6; rural China rising from under 0.1 in the 1990s to 0.5 to 0.6 by the late 2010s; sub-Saharan Africa plausibly 0.2 to 0.5, with wide uncertainty.

## Key question 2. In community surveys of stroke survivors and in care-seeking studies, what share were hospitalised, what barriers are named, and how large are the urban-rural, sex and wealth gaps?

### Takeaway
Among survivors, 60% were admitted in a rural Indian tea-estate population (2024-25) and 79% used some allopathic provider in rural South Africa (about 2001), while among people who died of stroke the share with any hospital contact was about half (56% in Faridabad, 48.5% formal care in Freetown). Cost, not recognising the event as serious, a first stop at a local or unqualified practitioner, and distance are the barriers named most often; belief in supernatural causation is documented for urban Dar-es-Salaam and assumed for Maputo.

### Inferences
- The barrier that shows up in numbers, as opposed to commentary, is the first stop at a provider who cannot treat stroke: a neighbourhood or unqualified practitioner in India, a primary-level government facility with no onward referral in Freetown, a village doctor in rural China. For the planned model this matters, because raising stroke-unit coverage among the admitted does nothing for people whose pathway ends at that first stop.
- Traditional healers are prominent in the African qualitative literature, but where counts exist they are a minority first resort (7 of 35 in Agincourt, 1 of 17 in Freetown) and are usually combined with allopathic care.
- Surveys of intention are a poor proxy: 85 to 88% say hospital, and observed contact among those who died is about half.

### Gaps
- No survivor survey in my set reports hospitalisation by sex or wealth, and only Tianjin reports it by age.
- I found no quantitative care-seeking study of stroke for the Middle East and North Africa, and none for Latin America beyond the registries.
- The Bangladeshi and Pakistani prevalence surveys I read do not ask where the stroke was treated.

## Key question 3. Is there evidence at two or more time points in the same LMIC population, or national-level data on the share of strokes treated in hospital?

### Takeaway
The only within-population series I could verify is the Tianjin Brain Study, where the admitted share of first-ever strokes rose from 6.9% (1992-2000) to 16.1% (2001-2009) to 54.2% (2010-2018), with the acceleration dated to the 2009 insurance reform. I found no national statistic for any LMIC that puts hospital stroke admissions over a community denominator.

### Cited Findings
- Tianjin by three periods, two periods, and four periods for women: rows A1 to A5. — [Han 2021](https://doi.org/10.2147/RMHP.S325822); [Liu 2021](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.11.08002); [Front Neurol 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2021.720962)
- Rate of change: "The hospitalization rate increased remarkably over the three periods for all sex, age, and stroke type groups, with an overall annual increase of 11.2%." The annual proportional change was 15.4% for those aged 65+ and 6.8% for those under 65. — [Han 2021](https://doi.org/10.2147/RMHP.S325822)
- Interrupted time series around 2009: "for hospitalization, the corresponding slopes for both periods were 0.011 (95% CI = 0.007, 0.015; P < 0.001) and 0.044 (95% CI = 0.010, 0.077; P = 0.017)", and "After 2009, a significant decline in the recurrence rate (P = 0.001) and a significant increase in the hospitalization rate (P = 0.004) were observed". — [Liu 2021](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.11.08002)
- Context for the Tianjin series: residents "had an annual per capita income of<$100 in 1991 and<$1000 in 2010", and "Tianjin's urban and rural residents’ medical insurance reached universal coverage after the 2009 health care reform". — [Liu 2021](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.11.08002)
- Early 2020: hospitalisation of first-ever strokes was 77.78% between 1 January and 20 April 2020, against 52.94% in the same weeks of 2019, on small numbers; the paper's own summary is that rates "remained stable across the study period". — [Liu 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2020.596871)
- Joinville, Brazil has two registries (1995 and 2005-06), but the first "was reported to be semi-populational based as survivors not hospitalised (third WHO module) were not included in the sample", so the pair cannot give a trend in the admitted share. — [Cabral 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp.2008.164475)
- Martinique has two registries (1998-99 and 2011-12); the first reports 6.5% not hospitalised, and the abstract of the second gives no figure. — [Smadja 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11739966/); [Olindo 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25213341/)
- Chile has two regions at two dates: 71% admitted in Iquique in 2000-02 and 85.7% in Ñuble in 2015-16. — [Lavados 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15978929/); [Lavados 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X%2820%2930470-8)
- National counts for China: 3,010,204 stroke admissions to 1,853 tertiary hospitals in the 2019 statistics report, and 3,418,432 admissions to 1,599 hospitals in 2020. — [China Stroke Statistics 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7548521/); [China stroke surveillance report 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10355019/)
- India's national registry programme (2018-19) reports sources of registration and a 7.1% death-certificate-only share but no admitted share. — [Rangamani 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2023.100308)

### Inferences
- The Tianjin slopes translate to about 1.1 percentage points a year before 2009 and 4.4 points a year after. If the later slope held, this township would pass 80% admitted in the mid-2020s; that is an extrapolation of mine, and the January-to-April 2020 value of 78% is consistent with it but rests on a few dozen cases.
- The Tianjin definition of "hospitalised" excludes township health centres, so the series is closer to "admitted to a hospital that could plausibly host a stroke unit" than most other rows in the tables. It is also one township of about 15,000 people, which the authors themselves say "is not representative of the overall rural population of China".
- The Chilean pair (71% then 86%) fits a rising trend but compares a northern port city with a poorer southern region, so the 15-point difference could be place as easily as time.
- The Chinese national admission counts are of the same order as published estimates of annual incident strokes, which fits a high admitted share in present-day China, but the counts include recurrent strokes, transfers and readmissions and cover only reporting hospitals. I would not turn them into a proportion.
- For India the only way to say anything about change is to set the mid-2000s urban registries (67 to 69%) beside the 2018-19 national programme, whose design cannot see untreated survivors. That is not a trend estimate.

### Gaps
- No repeated measure for any sub-Saharan African, Middle Eastern or South-East Asian population.
- The second Matão registry (2015-16) and second Martinique registry (2011-12) could supply two more within-population comparisons if their full texts report admission; I could not read them.
- No ministry report or national registry statement with a population denominator was found for India, Brazil, Mexico, Iran or South Africa.

## Key question 4. Is there a systematic review that pools the proportion of stroke patients hospitalised or seeking hospital care in LMICs or in sub-Saharan Africa?

### Takeaway
I found none. The reviews that touch the question either list a range from three Indian studies (26% to 69%) or assert, without data, that many African patients are managed at home.

### Cited Findings
- India: a systematic review of incidence, prevalence and case fatality found that only three studies reported hospitalisation rates, ranging from 26% to 69% (verbatim sentence under rows B2 and B3 above), and that the proportion undergoing CT or MRI ranged from 38% to 95%. No pooling was attempted. — [Jones 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/17474930211027834)
- A narrative summary inside a primary paper: "The proportion of stroke patients admitted to hospitals reported in recent population-based studies in developing countries is variable, ranging from 66% in Georgia[14] to as high as 95% in Brazil.[15]" — [Cantu-Brito 2011](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.110.592592)
- Stroke care in Africa (systematic review organised by phase of care): it reports timing among those who arrived ("less than 20% (26,34) of patients were admitted within three hours of stroke onset", where the bracketed numbers are reference markers) and lists "visit to traditional healers before coming to hospital, and treatment at home" among causes of late presentation, but gives no proportion of patients who never present. — [Urimubenshi 2018, PMID 29664359](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29664359/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1747493018772747), read from the [Glasgow repository copy](http://eprints.gla.ac.uk/161036/)
- Africa burden reviews make qualitative statements only: "the available hospital-based African studies underestimated stroke incidence as a result of exclusion of fatal or mild cases who do not present in these hospitals" — [Owolabi 2015, PMC4557491](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4557491/); "Many stroke patients have been managed at home due to lack of hospital funds, with only few presenting to standard health facilities several days after the onset of symptoms" — [Adeloye 2014, PMC4072632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4072632/)
- The World Stroke Organization and Lancet Neurology Commission cites delay, not non-presentation: "over 70% of people with stroke in Africa took more than 2 h to arrive at a hospital." — [Feigin 2023, PMID 37827183](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37827183/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422%2823%2900277-6)
- A claim that circulates without African data: a Nigerian prevalence paper says hospital-based studies are flawed "since close to one third of stroke patients may not get to hospital", and the reference it gives is a 1997 comparison of registries in Dijon, France. — [Sanya 2015, PMC4441143](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4441143/)
- Reviews whose full text I searched for statements on the share hospitalised, finding none: the Africa incidence and case-fatality meta-analysis ([Okekunle 2023, PMC10313746](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10313746/)), "Stroke in Africa: profile, progress, prospects and priorities" ([Akinyemi 2021, PMC8441961](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8441961/)), the Latin American incidence meta-analysis ([Nuñez 2023, PMC10547353](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10547353/)) and the 2026 narrative review of emergency medical services and prehospital stroke care in Africa ([PMC13772409](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13772409/)).

### Inferences
- A pooled estimate would have to be built from primary studies; the tables above are, as far as I can tell, the largest collection of such values for non-European LMICs, and they are too heterogeneous in numerator and denominator to pool without first sorting them by design.
- The "one third do not reach hospital" figure for Africa should not be cited as an African finding.

### Gaps
- "Stroke systems of care in low-income and middle-income countries" ([Pandian 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2820%2931374-X)) could not be read; no open copy was found in PubMed Central or the Glasgow and McMaster repository records.
- I did not find the Akinyemi or Owolabi papers named in the brief to contain a pooled proportion, but I read only those available in PubMed Central.

## Key question 5. Which studies of pre-hospital delay also quantify non-presentation?

### Takeaway
Almost none do. Delay studies recruit at the hospital door, and the few community-based designs that measure both delay and non-presentation (Faridabad deaths, Cachar survivors, rural Ludhiana) are already in the tables.

### Cited Findings
- Community denominators with both measures: Cachar survivors (39.6% never admitted; 50.0% admitted within 4.5 hours) — [Nambiar 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13504897/); Faridabad stroke deaths (43.8% visited no hospital; of all cardiac and stroke deaths "Only 10.8% of the subjects reached an appropriate health facility early") — [Krishnan 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2023.100222); rural Ludhiana (52.4% reached a facility within 4.5 hours; the share never reaching one is not in the abstract) — [Singh 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33951647/)
- Hospital-door denominators, which say nothing about those who never came: the Iquique figure that of 151 hospitalised ischaemic strokes "only 70 (38%) were assessed within 6 h" — [Lavados 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17239801/); the African review's finding that fewer than 20% of patients were admitted within three hours — [Urimubenshi 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1747493018772747); the Commission's "over 70% [...] took more than 2 h" — [Feigin 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422%2823%2900277-6); the WHO STEPS feasibility study in nine sites of five countries, which "focused on hospitalised stroke patients" and found "Half of all patients were hospitalised the same day." — [Truelsen 2007 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17239800/)
- The authors of the Faridabad study state the limit of a deaths-only design themselves: "since only deaths were covered this does not reflect the delays in all acute cardiac or stroke cases." — [Krishnan 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lansea.2023.100222)

### Inferences
- The delay literature for LMICs is large and should not be mined for the second factor. A sentence such as "52% arrived within 4.5 hours" has a different denominator in every study, and only in a community-based design can it be read alongside the share who never arrived.

### Gaps
- I did not screen the hospital-based delay literature study by study; the full-text searches I ran for phrases such as "never reach hospital" and "did not attend hospital" returned commentary, not counts.
