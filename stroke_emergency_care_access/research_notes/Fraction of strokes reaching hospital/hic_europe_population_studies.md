# Fraction of incident strokes admitted to hospital: population-based studies in high-income countries, all of Europe and the former Soviet Union, c. 1970 to 2025

Scope and method in brief. These notes cover high-income countries everywhere plus all of Europe and the former Soviet Union. Every percentage below was read in this session from a PubMed abstract (NCBI E-utilities and the PubMed connector), from open-access full text (Europe PMC / PMC XML), from an institutional-repository PDF, or from the WHO MONICA Data Centre web tables. According to PubMed, the abstracts say what is quoted; DOI or PubMed links are given for each article. Where I computed a percentage from published counts it is marked "derived" and the arithmetic is shown. The journal *Stroke* (ahajournals.org), *The Lancet* family and PMC HTML pages refused automated retrieval in this session (HTTP 403 or CAPTCHA), so many classic incidence papers could be read only as abstracts; the studies whose hospitalisation figure I could not retrieve are listed under "Could not verify" in the Gaps of Key Question 1.

Tier codes used in the tables. A: prospective population-based incidence study with multiple overlapping sources, including cases outside hospital. B: population-based register or cohort with a restriction (age band, survivors only, sample of practices, or methods not described in the abstract). C: administrative linkage, survey, or model-based estimate.

## Key Question 1: What percentage of incident strokes were hospitalised in each population-based study, and how did that change across repeated waves at the same site?

### Takeaway
Across 52 study-periods the share of incident strokes admitted to hospital runs from 34% (Tartu, 1970 to 1973) to 99% (Auckland, 2021 to 2022); in registers since 2000 it is 88% to 99% where stroke care is organised around admission (Auckland, South London, Dijon, Tartu, Grodno, Lund) and 54% to 71% where minor strokes are deliberately handled in rapid-access clinics, emergency departments or general practice (Oxfordshire, Porto, the Netherlands). Every site with repeated waves that started low rose (Auckland 62.5% to 98.9% over 40 years; Tartu 34% to 88% over 30 years; South London 82% to 94% over 15 years), while Oxfordshire moved only from 55% (1981 to 1986) to 63% (2002 to 2017) because outpatient assessment replaced home care rather than admission replacing it.

### Inferences
- "Hospitalised" is not one quantity across these studies. Rows 2 to 4, 30, 33 and 36 count a patient assessed in a rapid-access clinic or emergency department, imaged, and sent home as not hospitalised; rows 23 to 25 count emergency-department and coroner cases as hospital-ascertained; the Auckland series uses admission within 28 days, Oxfordshire 1981 within one month, Leicestershire within 7 days. For a stroke-unit model the admitted fraction is the relevant one, but the gap between "admitted" and "seen at hospital" is large exactly where outpatient pathways exist (Porto: 64% admitted, about 99% with hospital contact; OXVASC 2002 to 2007: 47.5% direct to hospital, 96% referred to secondary care).
- The denominators differ in a way that biases the percentage upward in some rows. Rows 5, 9, 27 and 35 exclude sudden deaths or include only survivors; most other abstracts do not say whether out-of-hospital deaths are counted. Where the split is explicit (Netherlands 2000, row 34), out-of-hospital deaths are 15% of all first strokes, so an "admitted" share computed on non-fatal or hospital-contact cases can overstate the share of all incident strokes by several points.
- Rows restricted to people under 65 or under 75 (rows 10, 11, 52 and most of Table 2) overstate the all-age figure, because every study that reports an age gradient finds lower admission at older ages.
- A defensible reading of the level for hospital-centred high-income systems is about 85% to 94% in the 1980s and 1990s (Dijon, Rochester, Umbria, Innherred, Martinique) and 91% to 99% from 2000 onward (Auckland, South London, Dijon, Lund), with general-practice-gatekept systems (UK, New Zealand, the Netherlands) starting much lower (55% to 73% in the 1980s and early 1990s).
- For Central and Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union the all-age evidence around 2000 splits into two groups: urban registers with strong hospital pathways (Tartu 88%, Grodno 90%) and settings where a third to a half or more stayed home (Uzhgorod 66%, Varna 46%, Krasnodar 40%). I would not pool them into one regional number without a covariate for rurality and age.

## Key Question 2: WHO MONICA stroke study (about 1982 to 1995): what share of non-fatal and fatal stroke events were managed outside hospital in each population?

### Takeaway
At ages 25 to 64, non-fatal events were managed in hospital in 98% to 100% of cases in Warsaw, Moscow, Friuli, Budapest, Pecs, Gothenburg and North Karelia, 92% to 98% in Glostrup, Turku/Loimaa, Rhein-Neckar, Northern Sweden and Kaunas, and 77% to 91% in Kuopio, East Germany, Novi Sad, Novosibirsk and Beijing; at ages 65 to 74 the share fell to 56% to 59% in Novosibirsk and 64% to 69% in East Germany. Fatal events were in hospital in 57% to 90% of cases where management was recorded, and the MONICA Data Centre itself warns that the non-hospitalised proportion reflects "the organization of medical care and perhaps ... the definition of the term 'hospital'" more than case-finding.

### Inferences
- A value near 100% for non-fatal events in a "cold pursuit" register (Warsaw, Moscow, Friuli) cannot distinguish universal admission from failure to look outside hospital; the Data Centre flags exactly this for Warsaw and Moscow. I would treat those rows as upper bounds, and treat Novosibirsk, Kaunas, East Germany, Novi Sad, Kuopio and Beijing, where out-of-hospital events were found in numbers, as the informative ones.
- The MONICA age window (25 to 64, sometimes to 74) removes the age group in which non-admission is most common. The drop between 25 to 64 and 65 to 74 is 15 to 24 points in East Germany and Novosibirsk and 9 points in Beijing, but only 2 to 3 points in Glostrup and Northern Sweden, so the age adjustment needed is itself system-dependent.
- The "all events" columns are my combination of the two tables and assume that a fatal event coded "in hospital" was managed in hospital in the sense relevant here. Where management is missing for many fatal events (Novosibirsk at ages 25 to 64) the combined figure is not usable; the 65 to 74 figures for Novosibirsk have almost no missing data and suggest roughly half of all events were managed in hospital.
- The within-MONICA trends point in different directions (Kaunas and Beijing up by 11 to 15 points over a decade, Novosibirsk control area down by 24 points from 1987 to 1993, Moscow with rising unattended deaths), which fits an economic-disruption effect in Russia in the early 1990s rather than a common secular trend.

### Gaps
- I could not open Asplund et al., Stroke 1995;26:355-360, or Thorvaldsen et al., Stroke 1995;26:361-367, in full (publisher returned HTTP 403), so the per-population figures above come from the Data Centre's later quality report (1998, covering more years) rather than from the tables of the 1995 papers; the 0% to 16% and 0% to 37% ranges are quoted second-hand.
- The quality report does not define "Other medical care" in the pages I read; it presumably covers home, outpatient and nursing-home care by a physician.
- Stegmayr's and Thorvaldsen's later MONICA papers were available only as abstracts, none of which carries management data.

## Key Question 3: Do systematic reviews tabulate or pool "percentage hospitalised" across population-based studies, and by country income level?

### Takeaway
I found no review that pools the percentage hospitalised or reports it by income level; what exists are ranges stated in passing: 82% to 98% across Italian registers, 56% to 91% across UK population-based studies, 5% and 15% non-hospital in two US studies, 0% to 16% non-fatal non-hospitalised events across MONICA populations, and 22% to 60% remaining in the community in a 1999 UK trial paper. The Feigin 2003 and 2009 reviews and the Sudlow and Warlow papers could not be opened, so whether their study-characteristics tables carry this column remains unverified.

### Cited Findings
- Italy: "Registers covered a total population of 2 262 940 people, with a hospitalization rate from 82% to 98%." — [Sacco 2011, Int J Stroke](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1747-4949.2011.00594.x)
- United Kingdom: "Hospital admission rates for acute stroke in population based studies in the United Kingdom have ranged from 56% to 91%." — [Addo 2011, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d744)
- United Kingdom, older statement: "Patients who have a stroke are not always admitted to hospital, and 22-60% remain in the community, frequently without coordinated rehabilitation." — [Walker 1999, Lancet](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10440303/)
- United States: "Two population-based stroke incidence studies, [,] reported that the proportion of patients with stroke without hospitalization (nonhospital strokes) was 5% and 15%, respectively." — [Williams 2001, BMC Neurol](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2377-1-2)
- General statement in a recent incidence paper: "Despite this evidence, 12–20% of stroke patients are not hospitalized". — [Abreu 2021, Front Neurol](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2021.685821)
- MONICA and Rotterdam as summarised in the discussion of a UK cohort paper: "In the Rotterdam Study [] only 53% of people with confirmed stroke were admitted to hospital." - Global Stroke Statistics (2017 edition, accepted manuscript) does not tabulate hospitalisation; its one sentence on the subject reads "hospitalization ratios differ between countries (e.g. '90% in Sweden,70 '10% in Japan).71 Therefore, registries may be a better proxy for important epidemiological measures in countries where the majority of incidence cases are treated in hospital." - The updated criteria for incidence studies require "maximally complete case ascertainment of both fatal and non-fatal, hospitalised and non-hospitalised new stroke events using multiple overlapping sources of information" but give no figure for the non-hospitalised share. — [Feigin 2018, Stroke, accepted manuscript](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/67322926/Updated_criteria_for_population_based_stroke_and_transient_ischemic_attack_incidence_studies_for_the_21st_century.pdf)

- The abstracts of the two Lancet Neurology reviews describe incidence, prevalence and case fatality only; neither mentions hospitalisation. — [Feigin 2003, Lancet Neurol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12849300/); [Feigin 2009, Lancet Neurol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19233729/)
- Three low- and middle-income figures surfaced in my abstract scan and belong to the other researcher's scope: Iquique, Chile, "151 (82%) patients were hospitalised"; Lagos, Nigeria, "Hospitalization rate was 84.6%"; Mumbai, India, "Two thirds of the FES cases were admitted to health care facilities ... the remaining 150 (32.8%) either died outside of hospital or were treated at home or nursing homes". — [Lavados 2007, Lancet Neurol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17239801/); [Danesi 2013, J Neurol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jns.2013.04.026); [Dalal 2008, J Assoc Physicians India](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19086353/)

### Inferences
- The Global Stroke Statistics sentence should not be read as "10% of strokes are hospitalised in Japan". Its two references are national registry papers, and the figures are almost certainly the registries' coverage of hospitalised strokes (the leading characters before "90%" and "10%" were lost in the PDF text, probably a tilde). I flag it because it is the only sentence in that review series that looks like an answer to this question.
- Since no review pools the quantity, Table 1 and Table 2 here are, as far as I can tell, the pooled evidence base for these regions; a meta-regression on calendar year, age window and a system-type indicator is feasible with about 50 to 70 points, though the definitions in the Inferences of Key Question 1 would have to be coded as covariates.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Feigin 2003, Feigin 2009, Sudlow and Warlow 1996 and 1997, Béjot 2016, Sacco 2011 and the Global Stroke Statistics 2014, 2020 and 2022 editions were not retrievable; I cannot rule out a hospitalisation column in their study-characteristics tables or web appendices. Sacco 2011 in particular probably tabulates the rate per Italian register (L'Aquila, Aosta, Belluno, Vibo Valentia, Umbria, Aeolian Islands and others), which would add six to eight rows.
- No review reporting the percentage hospitalised by World Bank income group was found in PubMed title and abstract searches.

## Key Question 4: Who is not admitted in high-income settings, what share die before any hospital contact (by subtype), and how much do hospital-based registers under-count?

### Takeaway
Three groups make up the non-admitted: people with minor strokes (65% of Porto's non-hospitalised had NIHSS under 5; 61% of OXVASC minor strokes went to an outpatient clinic), the very old and those in nursing homes (admission falls with age in every study that reports it, and 57% of Lund patients found only in primary care lived in care facilities), and people who die first (15% of all first strokes in the Netherlands, 6% of intracerebral haemorrhages, 13% to 24% of subarachnoid haemorrhages). Hospital-based registers and discharge data captured 63% to 91% of population-based cases in the Swedish and Norwegian linkage studies, and about 45% of incident strokes in Oxfordshire once non-admission (37%) and coding misses (29% of admissions) are combined (derived: 0.630 × 0.712).

### Cited Findings
Mild strokes and outpatient pathways:
- Porto: of 258 non-hospitalised first-ever strokes, 168 (65.1%) had NIHSS under 5 and only 10 (3.9%) were community-managed or did not attend the emergency department. — [Abreu 2021, Front Neurol](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2021.685821)
- Oxfordshire: "The outpatient clinic treated 40% of patients with stroke and 61% of those with minor stroke in our study population." — [Paul 2013, J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2012-303585)
- Oxfordshire 2002 to 2007: non-hospitalised versus hospitalised patients with TIA or stroke had major stroke in 13% versus 56% and a mean age of 73.1 versus 77.2 years. — [Pendlebury 2015, Stroke](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.114.008043)

- Kielce 1973 to 1974: "the incidence rates for CVD based exclusively on data from neurological departments are underestimated by at least 30 percent". — [Nowak 1979, Neurol Neurochir Pol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/514437/)
- Dijon 2013 to 2020: "18% of patients with ischemic CVE were managed as outpatients, and these patients would not have been identified through administrative hospital databases." — [Béjot 2025, J Am Heart Assoc](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.124.037925)
- Italy, hospital discharge records against chart review for ischaemic stroke: "Sensitivity of the test method was, therefore, 70.5% (95%CI, 68.0–73.0) and the PPV was 89.9%". — [Baldereschi 2018, PLoS One](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0193776)
- New Zealand, linked administrative data against a hospital stroke register: sensitivity 83% and positive predictive value 77%. — [Corbin 2026, Lancet Reg Health West Pac](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanwpc.2025.101768)
- Oxfordshire, on the method itself: "Ascertainment cannot be reliably assessed indirectly by statistical methods, such as capture-recapture", and direct checks found "Only 2 additional incident strokes" in the primary-care records of 90,542 people. — [Coull 2004, Stroke](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.STR.0000137605.48864.2f)
- Auckland, capture-recapture: estimated missing cases were 19.3%, 12.8%, 7.4%, 1.4% and 4.6% of collected cases in the five ARCOS studies. — [Feigin 2025, Lancet Reg Health West Pac](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanwpc.2025.101508)

### Inferences
- In today's high-income systems the non-admitted are mostly people who would gain least from a stroke unit (NIHSS under 5, seen and imaged the same day) or who could not gain (dead before arrival). The policy-relevant residue is smaller: the very old, care-home residents and people with dementia managed by a general practitioner or nursing-home physician, on the order of 3% in Lund in 2015 to 2016 and up to 20% in the Rotterdam sample.
- In lower-resource European settings around 2000 the selection ran the other way: the old, rural and severely affected stayed home and died there (Uzhgorod, Krasnodar, Varna). Scaling up admission in such settings would bring in patients with high baseline risk, for whom the absolute benefit of organised care could be larger than the trial average, but that is a conjecture the cited data cannot test.
- Pre-hospital death differs sharply by subtype, about 6% for intracerebral haemorrhage and 13% to 24% for subarachnoid haemorrhage, so a single "fraction admitted" applied to all strokes will overstate reach for subarachnoid haemorrhage. I found no population-based figure for pre-hospital death in ischaemic stroke alone; it must be well below the all-stroke 15% of the Dutch linkage, which mixes subtypes and counts deaths certified as stroke without hospital contact.
- The Catalan figure (62.5% of deaths outside hospital) is not a pre-hospital death rate: it counts every death certified as cerebrovascular outside an acute hospital, including deaths after discharge and in long-term care.

### Gaps
- No study in my set reports pre-hospital death separately for ischaemic stroke, and none reports the admitted fraction by pathological subtype as percentages (only odds ratios or contrasts).
- Evidence on nursing-home residents is thin: two Swedish and Dutch observations and the sampling design in Cincinnati. I found no population-based estimate of the admission rate for strokes occurring in care homes.
- Capture-recapture estimates exist for Auckland only in what I could read; the South London capture-recapture papers were not retrieved.

## Key Question 5: Is there direct evidence on the trend, and what covaries with it?

### Takeaway
Yes: four sites give within-site series under a constant protocol, and all show admission rising, by 36 points in Auckland (1981 to 2022), 54 points in Tartu (1970 to 2003) and 12 points in South London (1995 to 2012), with Dijon flat near 90% to 93%; the one clear counter-example is Oxfordshire, where admission went from 55% to only 63% because a same-day clinic absorbed minor strokes. The trend moves together with imaging and stroke-unit availability, public and professional messaging that stroke is an emergency, and the organisation of primary care, and it reversed in Russian MONICA populations in the early 1990s.

### Cited Findings
- Auckland, the authors' summary: "From 1981–1982 to 2021–2022, there was a significant increase in hospitalisations of people with stroke, brain CT/MRI within the first 7 days of hospital admission, and treatment in acute stroke units." — [Feigin 2025, Lancet Reg Health West Pac](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanwpc.2025.101508)
- Auckland, the association with survival: 28-day case fatality fell from 32% (1981 to 1982) to 23% (1991 to 1992) and 19% (2002 to 2003), and "further adjustment for care factors (higher hospital admission and neuroimaging) explained most of the improvement in survival." — [Carter 2007, Cerebrovasc Dis](https://doi.org/10.1159/000097054)
- Auckland 1991 to 1992, the authors' view of the then level: "management of acute stroke differed from clinical guidelines in the high proportion of patients managed in the community, the low rate of neurological consultation, and the low frequency of CT scanning." — [Anderson 1997, Aust N Z J Med](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1445-5994.1997.tb00965.x)
- South London: "There was a significant trend for increasing admission over time; 1995 to 2000 (82%), 2001 to 2006 (90%), and 2007 to 2012 (94%); P<0.001.", alongside "Rates of admission to stroke units and brain imaging, between 1995 and 2009, and for thrombolysis, between 2005 and 2009, increased significantly (P<0.001)." — [Bhalla 2016, Stroke](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.116.014136); [Addo 2011, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d744)
- Tartu: hospitalisation 34% in 1970 to 1973, 72% in 1991 to 1993 and 88% in 2001 to 2003, attributed by the author to "educational work since the 1970s introducing stroke as an emergency medical situation that needs to be managed in the hospital", and credited with part of the fall in 28-day case fatality from 49% to 30%: "probably as a result of increased hospitalisation (from 34 to 72%) the overall CFR decreased substantially." — [Vibo 2007, doctoral thesis](https://digar.ee/arhiiv/et/download/233212)
- Oxfordshire in the 1980s, the proposal that later became practice: "A stroke service that provides a facility for rapid outpatient and domiciliary diagnosis as well as a rapidly acting domiciliary nursing team might reduce the number of patients with stroke admitted to hospital without adversely affecting the quality of patient care". — [Bamford 1986, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.292.6532.1369)
- Oxfordshire since 2002: ascertainment includes a "daily rapid access "TIA and stroke clinic" to which participating GPs and the local emergency department refer individuals with suspected TIA or minor stroke", and 34.8% of all stroke episodes in 2002 to 2014 were "managed in clinic or at home". — [Li 2016, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2648)
- Rotterdam: "the share of unspecified strokes has decreased over the last three decades, from 75% in 1990 to 16% since 2016, indicating that patients are more often referred to a hospital for a stroke diagnostic workup than at the onset of the study." — [Berghout 2023, Lancet Reg Health Eur](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2023.100651)
- Cincinnati: the fall in white incidence between 1999 and 2005 "was also present when out-of-hospital ascertainment was included, which suggests that the decrease is not likely due to changes in admission rates vs. outpatient management of stroke patients." — [Kleindorfer 2010, Stroke](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.109.575043)
- Moscow, early 1990s: "the reorganization of primary care in Moscow, people do not go to hospitals as often as previously." — [MONICA quality report, section 8](https://www.thl.fi/publications/monica/strokeqa/strokeqa.htm)
- Kaunas 1986 to 1995 and Beijing 1984 to 1993: in-hospital share of non-fatal events at ages 25 to 64 rose from 82% to 97% and from 81% to 92%. — [MONICA quality report, Table 11](https://www.thl.fi/publications/monica/strokeqa/table11.htm)
- Zabrze, Poland, 2005 to 2006: the authors attribute lower case fatality to "improved management of acute stroke and hospitalizing all stroke patients", though their register was built from hospital-treated cases. — [Wawrzyńczyk 2011, Neurol Neurochir Pol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21384287/)
- Health-system organisation as the stated explanation for cross-sectional differences: Glostrup ("all patients suspected of having a stroke are admitted to a hospital"), Gothenburg ("in Sweden all suspect strokes are hospitalized"), Friuli ("all stroke patients are admitted to hospital"). — [MONICA quality report, section 8](https://www.thl.fi/publications/monica/strokeqa/strokeqa.htm)
- The admission decision in a gatekept system, Oxfordshire 1980s: in a substudy of 162 patients, "Sixty patients were admitted. The only reason for admission was diagnostic uncertainty in five cases (though this was a contributing factor in 25) and to provide nursing or general, non-medical care in 25." and "All 12 patients who presented directly to the casualty department were admitted, though only five had had a severe stroke." — [Bamford 1986, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.292.6532.1369)

### Inferences
- A working summary of level by era and system type, read off Tables 1 and 2 (my grouping, all ages unless stated): (a) admission-by-default systems in Western and Northern Europe, North America and the French territories, about 85% to 93% in the 1980s and 1990s and 91% to 99% after 2000; (b) general-practice-gatekept systems (UK, New Zealand, Netherlands, Australia in part), about 55% to 78% in the 1980s and early 1990s, 82% to 91% by 2000 to 2006, and 94% to 99% by 2010 to 2022 where admission is policy, but 54% to 71% where minor strokes go to clinics; (c) Central and Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union, 34% to 70% in the 1970s, 40% to 90% around 2000 with urban registers at the top, and under-65 MONICA figures of 77% to 100% for non-fatal events in the 1980s and 1990s.
- The caller's working example (55% in Oxfordshire in the 1980s rising to more than 90% today) holds for the UK as represented by South London (94% by 2007 to 2012) and for Auckland (99% by 2021 to 2022), but not for Oxfordshire itself (63%). The difference is definitional and organisational, not a difference in access.
- Candidate covariates the cited series support: calendar year; CT or MRI availability (Auckland 12% to 99% in step with admission); stroke-unit rollout (Auckland 12% to 79%, South London); the thrombolysis era and guidelines that make stroke an emergency; primary-care gatekeeping versus direct hospital access; age structure of the stroke population; rurality (Varna); economic and health-system disruption (Russia 1990 to 1993). None of the sources estimates these effects formally; the pairing is temporal.
- For the model's second factor, the fraction admitted is close to saturation (above 90%) in most high-income countries by the 2010s, so further stroke-unit gains there come from the third factor (coverage among the admitted), while for Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union the admitted fraction was still a binding constraint around 2000 and there is almost no population-based evidence after 2003 to say where it stands now.

### Gaps
- No within-site series could be assembled for Perth, Melbourne, Lund, Söderhamn, Frederiksberg, Erlangen, L'Aquila, Arcadia, Warsaw, Novosibirsk or the Japanese cohorts, although each has repeated waves; the figures sit in full texts I could not open.
- After 2003 there is no population-based observation of the admitted fraction anywhere in Central or Eastern Europe or the former Soviet Union in what I retrieved; Grodno and Tartu (2001 to 2003) are the latest.
- No source quantifies the contribution of each covariate; the associations above are concurrent trends within single sites.
- The Auckland 2002 to 2003 percentage is printed as 90.9% with 1,755 of 1,938 events, which computes to 90.6%; the small difference probably reflects missing data handling and does not change the picture.
